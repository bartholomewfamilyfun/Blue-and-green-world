<p>Teachers in Richland Parish, Louisiana, recently found bonuses of nearly $51,000 in their paychecks, up from about $10,200 a year earlier. The money came from construction-phase sales tax on Meta's $27 billion Hyperion data center. It will likely last three to five years. Then a thirty-year property tax abatement takes over.</p><p>That's the resource curse in miniature: a windfall arrives fast, terms are set once, and keeping the proceeds proves harder than winning them. Norway turned its oil into a fund worth roughly $2.3 trillion. Nigeria negotiated majority stakes and built a savings account that held $20 billion in 2007 — and $376,655 by June 2022. It owned the asset and still lost it.</p><p>Even Loudoun County, Virginia, where data centers supply 38% of the general fund, created a nearly $120 million stabilization fund to cushion revenue it can't control.</p><p>There's an alternative almost no one negotiates for. A data center's product isn't tax revenue. It's computing capacity, a physical asset inside the county line. A permit could reserve one or two percent of it for the community, permanently. Cash is spent. Capacity is held.</p><p>What would a rural parish do with a supercomputer? In the 1930s, utilities asked the same thing about farms. Nine in ten had no electricity, so the Rural Electrification Administration financed cooperatives to wire them anyway. Those locally owned co-ops still serve more than 42 million people. Or ask Michael Jordan, who earned about $94 million playing basketball, then sold his team at more than ten times the valuation he bought in at.</p><p>Ownership does the work.</p><p><strong>Computing power is only one of the assets a data center can put in a community's hands. Its retired hardware is some of the richest ore on Earth, and its waste heat could fuel entire industries.
