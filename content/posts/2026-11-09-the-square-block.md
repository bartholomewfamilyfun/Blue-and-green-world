<p>Every transformative mechanism reaches a moment when the people already on top try to cap it. After emancipation, that moment came fast.</p><p>Within a decade of freedom, formerly enslaved men were the elite riders in America's premier sporting event: thirteen of the fifteen jockeys at the first Kentucky Derby were Black. Then the sport grew lucrative, and they were pushed out. In 1908 Jack Johnson won the heavyweight title, and the country panicked. Two years later Congress passed the Mann Act — the "White Slave Act" — which was soon used to prosecute Johnson himself. Rhetoric from the highest levels of government and media was built to frighten and control.</p><p>The wheel of emancipation, promising equality, became a square block: something to crush with, and a foundation stone for someone else's plans.</p><p>This is the path of the technologies we fear now. The people positioned to benefit most are often as frightened as everyone else. They try to control the technology, stockpile its resources in a few hands, and promote progress only as far as it entrenches their position. It is the point at which corruption dominates and empires fall — finite people capping a technology's reach to fit the limits of their own lifespans.</p><p>Naming that demon matters. America only partially overcame it, but partially was enough to change the outcome. Naming it gives us better than accidental odds next time.</p><p><strong>Capping the outcome is only half the story. The other half is who paid for the alternative, and how the whole mechanism fits together.
