<p>Every transformative mechanism reaches a moment when the people already on top try to cap it. After emancipation, that moment came fast.</p><p>Within a decade of freedom, formerly enslaved men were the elite riders in America's premier sporting event: thirteen of the fifteen jockeys at the first Kentucky Derby were Black. Then the sport grew lucrative, and they were pushed out. In 1908 Jack Johnson won the heavyweight title, and the country panicked. Two years later Congress passed the Mann Act — the "White Slave Act" — which was soon used to prosecute Johnson himself. Rhetoric from the highest levels of government and media was built to frighten and control.</p><p>The wheel of emancipation, promising equality, became a square block: something to crush with, and a foundation stone for someone else's plans.</p><h2>The pattern in technology</h2><p>This is the path of the technologies we fear now. The people positioned to benefit most are often as frightened as everyone else. They try to control the technology, stockpile its resources in a few hands, and promote progress only as far as it entrenches their position. It is the point at which corruption dominates and empires fall — finite people capping a technology's reach to fit the limits of their own lifespans.</p><p>Naming that demon matters. America only partially overcame it, but partially was enough to change the outcome. Naming it gives us better than accidental odds next time.</p><h2>Who kept the lights on</h2><p>The second lesson is about partners. Shut out of the main system, Black athletes built parallel ones: the Negro Leagues, Black college athletics, a scouting and development pipeline outside the market. Someone still had to pay for it.</p><p>Jewish businessmen — members of a marginalized group fighting for its own footing — played an outsized role. Promoters and owners like Eddie Gottlieb and Abe Saperstein provided financial infrastructure the mainstream refused to. Saperstein, of Harlem Globetrotters fame, later launched a professional basketball league of his own. The partnership was transactional, complicated, and real.</p><p>We remember Jesse Owens, Jackie Robinson, and Dr. J. The ledger is incomplete without the people who kept the lights on: one marginalized group choosing commerce over the color line while the rest of the country chose contempt.</p><p>Decades later the pattern repeated. MTV, built to lead the future of music, barely played Black artists until CBS Records president Walter Yetnikoff threatened to pull his entire roster unless it aired Michael Jackson. One executive with leverage chose to spend it.</p><h2>The lesson</h2><p>Breakthroughs rarely come from the center. They come through cracks: arenas where gatekeeping fails, outsiders who fund what insiders won't, and individuals willing to spend their leverage on someone else's door. In 1865 those cracks were found by accident. With AI, gene editing, and surveillance still young, we can look for them on purpose.</p><hr />
