<p>If we don't learn from history, it starts with cures priced like mansions. The first CRISPR cure for sickle cell costs about $2.2 million. That's one reason only a few hundred of the 100,000 Americans with the disease have received it so far.</p><p>Then it moves from treatment to upgrades: denser bones, sharper memory, longer lives, sold first to those who can pay. Edits made to embryos pass to their children. A few generations later, the haves have engineered themselves into something new. The have-nots are left behind as the species' earlier draft, relative monkeys, in both senses. Darwin already told us we're related. The fear is that the rest of us become the relatives.</p><p>This isn't science fiction. In 2018, a Chinese scientist named He Jiankui announced the birth of twin girls whose embryos he had edited, and he was sent to prison. A $2.2 million therapy is a lottery ticket. An inherited upgrade is a dynasty.</p><p>But decency on a global scale has happened before. Smallpox was eradicated in 1980. Wild polio has been cut by more than 99 percent. PEPFAR, launched in 2003, is credited with saving 26 million lives, mostly of people who could never pay. Decency can also be reversed, so it has to keep being chosen.</p><p>If we do learn from history, the edits that already exist point somewhere extraordinary. Baby KJ Muldoon, born with a rare and often fatal liver disorder, received a CRISPR treatment designed for him alone in 2025 and is thriving. A healthy birth stops being luck.</p><p>Even space looks different. In microgravity, astronauts lose 1 to 1.5 percent of their bone mass a month, roughly what an aging adult on Earth loses in a year. But a rare variant of a gene called LRP5 gives some people unusually dense, fracture-resistant bones, partly because it resists sclerostin, a protein that puts the brakes on bone growth. In 2011 NASA sent mice to space to test a sclerostin blocker, and those results helped support FDA approval of romosozumab for osteoporosis. Tardigrades make a protein called Dsup that shields DNA from radiation, and researchers have delivered it into mice. All of this is early, but the possibilities are real: humans able to live on the Moon, on Mars or on a changing Earth.</p><p>Down one path, a select few rewrite themselves and start believing they're gods. Down a better path, we deliver a collective promise where every child starts with a working body. With history properly learned, gene editing takes us beyond.</p><p><strong>The future of gene editing depends on what we learn from those who went first.
