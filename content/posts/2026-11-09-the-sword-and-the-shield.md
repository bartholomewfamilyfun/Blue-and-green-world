<p>Start with the smallest society: the family. No one earns a place there through output. The infant contributes nothing and receives everything. That is the original social organism, and its logic is nurture.</p><p>Nurture doesn't scale cheaply. Beyond the band and the village, strangers outnumber kin, and care gives way to protection. Societies scaled through duty. You owed your labor and your life upward, to God and king, and received protection in return. The British Empire, on which the sun never set, was structurally <em>The Clan of the Cave Bear</em> with a navy.</p><p>Notice what happened in the scaling. The band existed to protect and feed the people in it. Duty reversed the arrow. Society became the thing to be served, and people became its parts — the serf, the soldier, the field hand, the factory hand — each valued for output. Strip away the steel and the satellites, and the organization looks familiar: an alpha, a hierarchy, a troop defending its territory. We built a space-age civilization and kept a primate org chart.</p><p>Frustration with duty produced the rights-based society, and it is a real achievement: walls no king may breach. But it rests on a contract, and a contract is adversarial by design — two parties, each owing the other the minimum.</p><p>Duty provides a sword to fight for society. Rights provide a shield to resist society. Neither ensures a sense of peace and ownership within society. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks drew the line: a contract governs interests; a covenant binds people through belonging.</p><p>A nurture-based society — a covenant — starts from a different premise. Society exists not to extract a purpose from you, nor merely to referee your disputes, but to help you become whoever you can become. In Pixar's <em>Soul</em>, unborn souls must find a "spark" before entering life. The hero assumes a spark must be a calling. The movie posits that it is only a readiness to live. A nurture-based society runs that seminar with no tuition or entrance exam.</p><p>1865 shows what happens without it. Emancipation delivered rights without nurture. Sherman's Field Order 15 promised land; by the end of 1865 Andrew Johnson had begun handing it back to former Confederates. The freedmen held a contract and an empty hand. Cultural sovereignty emerged only from the cracks where nurture survived anyway: the church, the HBCU, the porters' union, the ballfield.</p><p><strong>A nurture society sounds soft until you see what it produces.
