<p>Every previous wave of automation came with an escape hatch. The loom took the weaver's hands, so the weaver moved to the factory. The tractor took the farmhand's back, so the farmhand moved to the city. For two centuries the answer to automation was the same: think for a living. Now thinking is the thing being automated.</p><p>So the question has to be asked plainly. Fifty years from now, can we find any areas of growth for humans? Or are humans just holding on?</p><p>The fear isn't poverty. Societies have solved poverty before. The fear is irrelevance — what the historian Yuval Noah Harari called the "useless class." It is one thing to be poor in a society that needs you. It is another to be comfortable in a society that doesn't.</p><p>And the stakes are political. Nearly every expansion of rights was purchased with leverage, and the leverage was usefulness. Kings conceded to parliaments because they needed taxes. Industrialists conceded to unions because a strike works only if the work stops. In 1865 the strongest argument for Black citizenship was the roughly 180,000 Black soldiers who had worn the Union uniform. Remove the need and you remove the leverage.</p><p>There is an irony here. For centuries, duty societies tried to turn people into machines — faceless Georges, punctual, interchangeable, tireless — and complained when people refused to stay that way. Now we are building actual machines to replace the lazy humans who never quite managed to behave like machines.</p><p>That is why automation frightens us. We still think in the logic of a duty society, where your worth is your output. In that logic, the robots aren't coming for our jobs. They're coming for our serfdom.</p><p>If our only claim is usefulness, then yes, we are holding on, and the grip is slipping. But usefulness was never the right claim.</p><p><strong>If usefulness isn't our claim, what is?
