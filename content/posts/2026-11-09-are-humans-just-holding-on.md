<p>Every previous wave of automation came with an escape hatch. The loom took the weaver's hands, so the weaver moved to the factory. The tractor took the farmhand's back, so the farmhand moved to the city. For two centuries the answer to automation was the same: think for a living.</p><p>Now thinking is the thing being automated. So the question has to be asked plainly: fifty years from now, can we find any areas of growth for humans? Or are humans just holding on?</p><h2>The real fear</h2><p>The fear isn't poverty. Societies have solved poverty before, unevenly and late. The fear is irrelevance. It is one thing to be poor in a society that needs you. It is another to be comfortable in a society that doesn't.</p><p>The stakes are political. Nearly every expansion of rights was bought with leverage, and the leverage was usefulness. Unions won because a strike works only if the work stops. In 1865 the strongest argument for Black citizenship was the roughly 180,000 Black soldiers who had served the Union. The republic had needed them, and need created a debt.</p><p>Remove the need and you remove the leverage. States that draw wealth from oil rather than citizens rarely answer to citizens. Institutions that draw wealth from compute face the same temptation. If our only claim is usefulness, then yes — we are holding on.</p><p>But usefulness was never the right claim.</p><h2>Duty, rights, nurture</h2><p>The smallest society is the family, and no one earns a place there through output. The infant contributes nothing and receives everything. Its logic is nurture.</p><p>Nurture didn't scale, so societies scaled through duty: you owed your labor and life to God and king in exchange for protection. Frustration with that produced the rights-based society — a real achievement, but one built on a contract. Contracts are adversarial by design: two parties, each owing the other the minimum. Rights say what can't be done to you. They're silent on what you're for.</p><p>A nurture-based society — a covenant — starts elsewhere. Society exists to help each person become whoever they can become, without defining that in advance. In Pixar's <em>Soul</em>, unborn souls must find a "spark" before life, and the hero assumes it's a calling. The film's answer is that it was only ever readiness to live.</p><p>1865 shows what happens without nurture. Emancipation delivered rights, and Sherman's Field Order 15 promised land — then Andrew Johnson began handing the land back to former Confederates. Freedom arrived as a contract and an empty hand. Yet the cultural sovereignty that followed grew exactly where nurture survived: the church, the HBCU, the porters' union, the ballfield.</p><h2>Why now</h2><p>Nurture at scale was never affordable. Duty societies needed your body. Rights societies needed your work. A society that no longer needs your labor is the first in history that can afford to care whether you flourish. The machine that threatens to make us unnecessary is also the surplus that makes nurture possible.</p><p>So what would humans do? Craft, learn, entertain, engineer, and explore — not because we'll beat the machines, but because human limits are the point. A computer beat the world chess champion in 1997, and chess is more popular now than ever. A novel matters because someone mortal wrote it anyway.</p><p>And output stops being the purpose. Someone studies physics because the universe is astonishing; if she invents the material for an orbital skyhook, that's extra. Someone writes to become more fully themselves; if the book is extraordinary, that's extra too.</p><p>One caution: "for your own good" was also the language of the boarding school. Nurture without rights is paternalism. Rights are the floor. Nurture is the house.</p><p>Are humans just holding on? Only if we keep asking to be needed. The future isn't empty. It's unnamed. And for the first time, it can afford us.</p>
