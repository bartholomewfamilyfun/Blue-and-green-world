<p>The worst lesson to take from Henrietta Lacks or Tuskegee is shame, the kind that makes people avoid history or remember it once a year in February as a wound. And no part of American history is more misunderstood, especially by the people it supposedly insults, than the three-fifths clause of the U.S. Constitution.</p><p>Many Americans believe the Constitution declared black people three-fifths of a human being. It didn't. The clause decided how to count population for seats in Congress: "the whole Number of free Persons" plus "three fifths of all other Persons." Free black Americans were counted in full, exactly like free white Americans. "Other persons" is not a euphemism for black people.</p><p>Here's a further twist. The slaveholders wanted enslaved people counted in full. That wasn't respect. More people meant more seats in Congress, more electoral votes and more power for the slaveholding South. Northern delegates would have preferred enslaved people not be counted at all, to stop slaveholders from turning human property into political power. The three-fifths compromise was not a diminution of black people. It weakened the political power of the slave-owning South.</p><p>The proper lesson of history is almost never shame. It's interdependence. The sick feeling you get when you think about Tuskegee isn't a burden to carry. It's evidence that you already know what those doctors forgot. We are bound to each other, and harm to one is harm that affects us all in one way or another.</p><p>Society began as a clever way to protect and nurture its members. Somewhere along the way the arrow flipped. Society became the machine to be served, and people became its parts, each valued for output. Gene editing poses the same fork, only deeper. This time the question isn't who does the work. It's who gets to be called human.</p><p><strong>The three-fifths clause shows how history gets misread.
