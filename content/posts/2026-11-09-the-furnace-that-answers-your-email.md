<p>Every watt of electricity that enters a data center leaves as heat. Not most of it — essentially all of it. A 100-megawatt facility is a 100-megawatt furnace that happens to answer your email. The only question is whether that heat warms a community or warms the sky.</p><p>Finland chose the community. Outside Helsinki, Microsoft and Fortum built the world's largest data center heat-recovery system. Heat pumps lift 30°C server water to 86°C and feed it into a district heating network serving about 250,000 people — roughly 40% of their annual heating demand, with an expected 400,000 tonnes of CO2 avoided each year.</p><p>It worked because two conditions existed: cold air, and pipes. Espoo's district heating system dates to 1954. Microsoft didn't build a heating system. It plugged into one.</p><p>America has pipes too. Con Edison's Manhattan steam network has run since 1882 and is larger than the next nine systems in the world combined. But it runs at roughly 450°F, and server heat tops out near 187°F even after heat pumps. Wrong pipes.</p><p>So the American opportunity isn't heating homes. It's industrial. Greenhouses want heat in July. So do fish farms, food dehydrators, pharmaceutical dryers, and industrial laundries. Put them beside a data center, and one tenant's biggest overhead becomes another's fuel. History favors the move: gasoline, dynamite, and natural gas all began as discarded byproducts.</p><p>The catch is timing. A heat connection is cheap while the trenches are open for power and fiber, and nearly impossible once the site is finished.</p><p><strong>Heat is just one of the windfalls data centers can deliver. Their retired servers hide some of the richest ore on Earth, and their computing capacity could become a community's most valuable asset.
