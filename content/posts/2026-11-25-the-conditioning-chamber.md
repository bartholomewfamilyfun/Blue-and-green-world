<p>Try posting a comment the platform doesn't want, and you'll find out quickly that the free exchange of ideas has terms of service. I watched this happen on YouTube during the Sean Combs scandal. For two years, videos and comments mocked him with homophobic slurs, and creators boosted their careers with bits questioning his sexuality, on a platform whose rules prohibit exactly that. When I posted a comment defending him as a human being (not his crimes, his humanity), it wouldn't post. But the insults did.</p><p>None of this breaks the law. In 2020, the Ninth Circuit ruled that despite YouTube's ubiquity and its role as a public-facing platform, it remains a private forum, not subject to the First Amendment. The wall is legal.</p><p>People call social media an echo chamber, and the phrase is too gentle. An echo chamber only repeats you. This chamber punishes some speech, rewards other speech, and feeds you whatever keeps you agitated. Do that long enough and it doesn't reinforce your opinion; it installs one. It's a Pavlovian conditioning chamber.</p><p>The real test of mind control isn't whether you can make people believe something. It's whether, after they believe it, you can make them do the opposite on command, without anyone noticing the switch. Orwell wrote that scene. During Hate Week in <em>1984</em>, a speaker denouncing the enemy is handed a note, and mid-sentence the enemy changes. The crowd turns its fury on the new target, and nobody admits the old one was ever different.</p><p>The Sean Combs case worked as that kind of test. For a decade we have been taught, correctly, that mocking a person's sexuality is cruel. Then the wall marked one man as rotten meat, and the same people who had learned the rule broke it with enthusiasm, without penalty, and without noticing the contradiction. A rule that can be suspended on cue was never a principle; it was a setting.</p><p><strong>The chamber runs on money, and it reaches into the past.
