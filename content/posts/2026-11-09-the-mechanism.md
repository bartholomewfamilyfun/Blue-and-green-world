<p>Every week brings another headline: AI is coming for your job, automation is coming for your industry. The labor market is shifting, and quickly. But the fear that the machines are coming for us isn't new. It's wearing a new outfit.</p><p>The last time an entire class of people had zero leverage, zero capital, and every institutional door closed, the outcome 150 years later wasn't collapse. It was partnership in some of the largest industries on the planet. That isn't a feel-good story. It's a mechanism — and a mechanism can be studied, repeated, and improved.</p><h2>The exercise</h2><p>To understand what an unrecognizable technology will do in fifty years, don't stare at the technology. Find a moment when the outcome was unimaginable to the people living through it, and reverse-engineer how it happened. In eight steps:</p><ol><li><p><strong>Powerless at the precipice.</strong> In 1865, four million people were freed. For the first time, their labor belonged to them.</p></li><li><p><strong>Dismal prospects.</strong> No land, no capital, restricted education, gatekept professions. Every high-status path blocked.</p></li><li><p><strong>Opportunity presents.</strong> Talent flows to the arenas where gatekeeping fails. You can deny a loan quietly; you can't deny who crossed the finish line first. Thirteen of the fifteen jockeys in the first Kentucky Derby, in 1875, were Black.</p></li><li><p><strong>The mechanism is perverted.</strong> Black jockeys were pushed out. Jack Johnson's 1908 heavyweight title triggered a national panic, and the Mann Act followed. Those on top tried to cap the outcome.</p></li><li><p><strong>Concentration of resources.</strong> The Great Migration built density in northern cities, and basketball — invented in 1891, needing almost no land or equipment — landed in the communities with the least capital.</p></li><li><p><strong>Heroes arise.</strong> Segregation forced parallel institutions: the Negro Leagues, Black college athletics. Jewish promoters like Eddie Gottlieb and Abe Saperstein, outsiders themselves, helped fund them when the mainstream wouldn't.</p></li><li><p><strong>Mechanisms feed mechanisms.</strong> MTV launched as a forward-looking network that barely played Black artists — until CBS Records president Walter Yetnikoff threatened to pull his label's roster unless it aired Michael Jackson.</p></li><li><p><strong>The impossible future.</strong> From spirituals in a cotton field to Marian Anderson singing at the Lincoln Memorial. The shackled become the sovereign.</p></li></ol><h2>Why it matters now</h2><p>No one in 1865 could have predicted the present — not for lack of imagination, but because the scale of what followed was unfathomable. Imagining a gifted athlete was easy. Imagining an industry was not.</p><p>And it could have gone differently. 1865 to today is one branch on a tree of outcomes, most of them tragic. India's Dalits faced exclusion just as total, and the world was never offered the same story. Both outcomes are logical. America's came down to specific cracks, specific choices, and specific people — mostly by accident.</p><p>That's the series. Each episode takes a frightening technology and asks: What crack is nobody pricing? Who generates the value, and who captures it? What's being suppressed? And what would a fantastic, realistic outcome require? We don't have to hope we live in the universe that turns out fine. We can build it.</p><hr />
