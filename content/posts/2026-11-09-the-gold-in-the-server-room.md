<p>Everyone is arguing about whether to allow data centers. Almost no one is asking what's inside them.</p><p>A productive gold mine processes rock holding one to five grams of gold per tonne. A tonne of high-grade circuit boards holds 150 to 400. A retired server isn't junk. It's ore of exceptional grade — already mined, refined, catalogued, and stacked under a roof.</p><p>And the mine refills. The buildings last fifteen to twenty years, but AI chips are outpaced in eighteen to thirty-six months. The first big wave, bought during the 2022–2024 buildout, is being decommissioned between now and 2029.</p><p>Virginia, home to the densest concentration of compute on Earth, exempts that hardware from sales tax — about $1 billion in fiscal 2024, with five companies receiving 82% of the benefit. Its 2026 legislature considered sixty-one data center bills. None addressed the metal. Meanwhile, the refining industry is rising in South Carolina, where one planned magnet plant is expected to employ about 490 people. The deposit sits in one state; the value lands in another. And with China dominating rare-earth refining, this is a question of national sovereignty as much as local economics.</p><p>In 1865, freed people were denied the asset by design: no seat, no counsel, no vote. Virginia has every one of those tools. The asset isn't being denied. It's being left unrequested.</p><p>The metal is coming out either way. The only question is whether anyone writes a clause about where it goes before the trucks are loaded.</p><p><strong>The metal is only the first of three assets most communities are leaving on the table. Every server rack also throws off heat that could fuel whole industries, and the computing power itself may be the most valuable prize of all.
