<p>In 2019, Victoria Gray, a mother of four from Mississippi, became the first person with sickle cell disease treated with CRISPR. Doctors removed stem cells from her bone marrow, edited a single gene and infused more than two billion edited cells back into her body. In late 2023 the treatment, Casgevy, became the first CRISPR medicine approved anywhere.</p><p>The first CRISPR cure didn't go to sickle cell because white people wanted to "try it out" on black people first. But it wasn't a goodwill gesture either. Sickle cell went first because it was the easiest target in medicine. One well-understood defect causes it, and the cells that need fixing live in the bone marrow, where they can be taken out, edited in a lab and put back. Cystic fibrosis affects the lungs and other organs that can't be taken out.</p><p>But let's not engage in gaslighting. Black Americans have a right to be pessimistic, because they have been first in line before.</p><p>In 1951, doctors at Johns Hopkins took tissue from the tumor of Henrietta Lacks, a 31-year-old black mother of five, without her knowledge or consent. Her cells kept dividing in the lab, indefinitely. They became the workhorse of modern biology, used in developing the polio vaccine, in vitro fertilization and the COVID-19 vaccines. She was buried in an unmarked grave.</p><p>In 1932, the U.S. Public Health Service began a study of 600 black men in Macon County, Alabama, 399 of them with syphilis. By 1943, penicillin was the standard cure. The men were never offered it. The study ran for forty years.</p><p>Here's a detail that rarely makes the story. In those same years, at the same Tuskegee Institute, the Army was training the Tuskegee Airmen, black pilots who became one of the most celebrated units of World War II. One town, two stories. The most shameful study in American medicine and some of the proudest service in American military history, side by side.</p><p>Notice the difference between Lacks and Gray. Lacks was never asked. Gray volunteered, signed a consent form and was told the risks. The outrage over Tuskegee produced the National Research Act of 1974 and the system of informed consent that governed her trial. The worst of history built the guardrails for the best.</p><p><strong>Why is the betrayal so much better remembered than the triumph? And what happens when a cure becomes an upgrade?
