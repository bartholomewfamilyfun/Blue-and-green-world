<p>In <em>1984</em>, the Ministry of Truth controlled the future by controlling the past. Whenever the story changed, clerks rewrote the old newspapers and books to match. Memories fade, witnesses die, and what children read in school becomes what happened.</p><p>Americans treat that as dystopian fiction. But it's American history. After Reconstruction, Confederate memorial groups rewrote the war in the nation's schoolbooks: the freedmen became corrupt, Reconstruction a disaster, and the Klan redeemers. By 1940, that version dominated classrooms nationwide. As late as 1961, an Alabama textbook called slavery the earliest form of social security in the United States.</p><p>But Americans took the truth back. Black historians challenged the myth as early as 1913, and black parents and grandparents corrected the lessons their children brought home. In 1926, the historian Carter G. Woodson launched Negro History Week. It grew into Black History Month. When Mississippi refused to approve an honest state history textbook, its authors sued and won, and in 1980 a federal judge ordered the state to approve it. It's proof that an entire system of lies can be corrected by decent Americans who refuse to forget.</p><p>But for all George Orwell's prescience, what he could not foresee was Wikipedia. Its administrators can delete pages and hide individual revisions from public view. A handful of Wikipedians can undo history with the click of a button. Unlike the post-Reconstruction textbooks, which could be answered with honestly written books, Wikipedia is part of the wall. You can present a true version if you like. But as long as people allow their attention to be directed by the wall, your perspective will remain invisible.</p><p>That's the price of a wall no one can see. People who can't tell what's true stop trusting anything. In 1958, 73% of Americans trusted the federal government to do the right thing most of the time; by 2024, 22%.</p><p><strong>This is the last of three Episode 5 quick reads.
