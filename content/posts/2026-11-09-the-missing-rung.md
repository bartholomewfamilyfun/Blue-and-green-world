<p>AI isn't coming for your job. It's coming for the job you would have had first.</p><p>For a century, the first years of a lawyer's career looked like drudgery: document review, first drafts, research memos no partner would read past page two. Firms billed it, associates resented it, and everyone understood that the bottom rung was where young lawyers learned to climb to the top.</p><p>Now the bottom rung is being removed. Lateral associates — lawyers trained somewhere else, at someone else's expense — make up 51% of associate hires at the 200 largest U.S. firms. Most of those firms plan to grow their associate ranks through 2027, but only about a third plan to grow their first-year classes. They still want experienced lawyers. They just don't want to train them. If no one climbs the bottom rung, where does the top of the ladder come from in twenty years?</p><p>The pattern runs well beyond law. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab found that employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is roughly 19% below where it would be had it tracked their peers in less-exposed jobs. Experienced workers in those same occupations show no comparable decline.</p><p>The reason is the difference between codified and tacit knowledge. Codified knowledge lives in textbooks and manuals; it's what young workers arrive with, and it's what AI reproduces best. Tacit knowledge is judgment built through years of doing. A company hires junior analysts and senior analysts. The machine can replace only one of them.</p><p>The displacement is real but quiet. Recent-graduate unemployment hasn't spiked, and plenty of "AI layoffs" are ordinary cost-cutting in disguise. It isn't a flood. It's a drain: fewer people entering, not necessarily more people leaving. Drains are quieter than floods, and easier to ignore until the tank is empty.</p><p><strong>Losing the first rung is only the beginning.
