<p>AI isn't coming for your job. It's coming for the job you would have had first.</p><p>The clearest evidence comes from payroll records. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab found that employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is now roughly 19% below where it would be had it tracked their peers in less-exposed jobs. Experienced workers in those same occupations show no comparable decline.</p><p>The reason is the difference between codified and tacit knowledge. Codified knowledge lives in textbooks and manuals — it's what young workers arrive with, and it's what AI reproduces best. Tacit knowledge is judgment built through years of doing. The machine can replace the junior analyst. It can't yet replace the senior one.</p><p>Law shows the pattern most sharply. Lateral associates — lawyers trained somewhere else, at someone else's expense — now make up 51% of associate hires at the 200 largest U.S. firms. Most of those firms plan to grow their associate ranks through 2027, but only about a third plan to grow their first-year classes. They still want experienced lawyers. They've stopped wanting to make them.</p><p>That's the hidden cost. Document review and first drafts were never busywork. They were tuition. Remove the bottom rung, and you have to ask where the top of the ladder comes from in twenty years.</p><p>The displacement is real but quiet. Recent-graduate unemployment hasn't spiked, and plenty of "AI layoffs" are ordinary cost-cutting in costume. This isn't a flood. It's a drain: fewer people entering, not more people leaving.</p><h2>The 1865 parallel</h2><p>In 1865, one of the first things Mississippi did with emancipation was regulate the ladder. Its Black Code allowed courts to bind Black minors as "apprentices" — with preference given to their former owners. Training became the instrument of recapture. Whoever controls the ladder controls the class.</p><p>Today's version is gentler, but the structure rhymes. The models replacing junior professionals were trained on the work of professionals who were never asked. When authors sued over pirated books used in AI training, they won a $1.5 billion settlement — about $3,000 a book. But the court had already ruled that training on lawfully acquired books is fair use. The authors were paid once. The knowledge stays in the model.</p><h2>Three ways to hold value</h2><p><strong>Wage.</strong> Experts are paid well to train AI right now. It's temporary by design: once the model learns, the job ends.</p><p><strong>Moat.</strong> Licenses protect professions — AI won't pass the bar. But a license protects the job, not the ownership. Arizona now lets non-lawyers own law firms, and in 2025 KPMG became the first Big Four firm cleared to practice law in the U.S. The moat stays. The castle changes hands.</p><p><strong>Deed.</strong> Lasting value comes from ownership:</p><ul><li><strong>Rights, not fees.</strong> Songwriters formed ASCAP in 1914 to license their work collectively, and they've collected royalties ever since. Professions could do the same with their expertise.</li><li><strong>Owning the firm.</strong> One professional with AI has the reach of a small firm. The practice is the forty acres.</li><li><strong>Accountability.</strong> AI can't owe a fiduciary duty or be sued for malpractice. Someone must sign, and that signature stays human.</li></ul><h2>The point</h2><p>The Pullman porters were handed a job designed as a trap — low wages, tips, and a uniform that made every man answer to "George." They organized it into the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, won a landmark contract in 1937, and anchored a Black middle class. Every new machine needs a human layer. The question isn't whether that layer exists. It's whether the people in it are organized, and whether they own anything.</p><p>The machine will pay you to teach it. The question is whether you own anything it learned.</p>
