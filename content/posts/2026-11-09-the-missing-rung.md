<p>AI isn't coming for your job. It's coming for the job you would have had first.</p><p>The clearest evidence comes from payroll records. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab found that employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is now roughly 19% below where it would be had it tracked their peers in less-exposed jobs. Experienced workers in those same occupations show no comparable decline.</p><p>The reason is the difference between codified and tacit knowledge. Codified knowledge lives in textbooks and manuals — it's what young workers arrive with, and it's what AI reproduces best. Tacit knowledge is judgment built through years of doing. The machine can replace the junior analyst. It can't yet replace the senior one.</p><p>Law shows the pattern most sharply. Lateral associates — lawyers trained somewhere else, at someone else's expense — now make up 51% of associate hires at the 200 largest U.S. firms. Most of those firms plan to grow their associate ranks through 2027, but only about a third plan to grow their first-year classes. They still want experienced lawyers. They've stopped wanting to make them.</p><p>That's the hidden cost. Document review and first drafts were never busywork. They were tuition. Remove the bottom rung, and you have to ask where the top of the ladder comes from in twenty years.</p><p>The displacement is real but quiet. Recent-graduate unemployment hasn't spiked, and plenty of "AI layoffs" are ordinary cost-cutting in costume. This isn't a flood. It's a drain: fewer people entering, not more people leaving.</p><p><strong>Losing the first rung is only half of it. In 1865 the ladder itself was a weapon, and today there are three ways to hold value.
