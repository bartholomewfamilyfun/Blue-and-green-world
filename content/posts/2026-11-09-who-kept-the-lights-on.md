<p>The second lesson is about partners. Shut out of the main system, Black athletes built parallel ones: the Negro Leagues, Black college athletics, a scouting and development pipeline outside the market. Someone still had to pay for it.</p><p>Jewish businessmen — members of a marginalized group fighting for its own footing — played an outsized role. Promoters and owners like Eddie Gottlieb and Abe Saperstein provided financial infrastructure the mainstream refused to. Saperstein, of Harlem Globetrotters fame, later launched a professional basketball league of his own. The partnership was transactional, complicated, and real.</p><p>We remember Jesse Owens, Jackie Robinson, and Dr. J. The ledger is incomplete without the people who kept the lights on: one marginalized group choosing commerce over the color line while the rest of the country chose contempt.</p><p>Decades later the pattern repeated. MTV, built to lead the future of music, barely played Black artists until CBS Records president Walter Yetnikoff threatened to pull his entire roster unless it aired Michael Jackson. One executive with leverage chose to spend it.</p><p>Breakthroughs rarely come from the center. They come through cracks: arenas where gatekeeping fails, outsiders who fund what insiders won't, and individuals willing to spend their leverage on someone else's door. In 1865 those cracks were found by accident. With AI, gene editing, and surveillance still young, we can look for them on purpose.</p><p><strong>The cracks are where a frightening technology can still be steered. See how the mechanism works, and how the powerful try to cap it.
