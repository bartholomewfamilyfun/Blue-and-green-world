<p>In 1865, one of the first things Mississippi did with emancipation was regulate the ladder. Its Black Code let courts bind Black minors as "apprentices," with preference given to their former owners. Training became the instrument of recapture. Whoever controls the training controls the class.</p><p>Today's version is gentler, but the structure rhymes. The models replacing junior professionals were trained on the work of professionals who were never asked. When authors sued Anthropic over pirated books, the result was a $1.5 billion settlement, about $3,000 a book. But the court had already ruled that training on lawfully acquired books is fair use. The authors were paid once, for the piracy. The knowledge stays in the model.</p><p>So how do knowledge workers hold value? At three levels, and only one of them lasts.</p><p><strong>Wage.</strong> Experts are paid well to train AI right now — lawyers, doctors, and coders grading model outputs. It's temporary by design. Once the model learns, the job ends, and the model keeps the knowledge forever.</p><p><strong>Moat.</strong> Licenses protect professions, and AI won't be admitted to the bar. But a license protects the job, not the ownership. Arizona now lets non-lawyers own law firms, and in 2025 KPMG became the first Big Four firm cleared to practice law in the U.S. The likely endgame: a few licensed lawyers, a fleet of AI, and investors holding the equity. The moat stays. The castle changes hands.</p><p><strong>Deed.</strong> Lasting value comes from ownership: rights, not fees (songwriters formed ASCAP in 1914 and have collected royalties ever since); owning the firm, since one professional with AI has the reach of a small firm; and accountability, because AI can't owe a fiduciary duty, and someone must sign.</p><p>History offers a model. The Pullman porters were handed a job designed as a trap: low wages, tips, and a uniform that made every man answer to the name "George" — the forerunner to Hey Google, Siri, and Claude. The porters organized it into the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, won a landmark contract in 1937, and anchored a Black middle class. Every new machine needs a human layer. The question is whether the people in it become organized porters or scattered staff.</p><p><strong>Ownership helps people keep value. The bigger question is what humans are for when the machine does the work.
