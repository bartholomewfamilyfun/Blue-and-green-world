<p>In 1865, one of the first things Mississippi did with emancipation was regulate the ladder. Its Black Code allowed courts to bind Black minors as "apprentices" — with preference given to their former owners. Training became the instrument of recapture. Whoever controls the ladder controls the class.</p><p>Today's version is gentler, but the structure rhymes. The models replacing junior professionals were trained on the work of professionals who were never asked. When authors sued over pirated books used in AI training, they won a $1.5 billion settlement — about $3,000 a book. But the court had already ruled that training on lawfully acquired books is fair use. The authors were paid once. The knowledge stays in the model.</p><p>There are three ways to hold value.</p><p><strong>Wage.</strong> Experts are paid well to train AI right now. It's temporary by design: once the model learns, the job ends.</p><p><strong>Moat.</strong> Licenses protect professions — AI won't pass the bar. But a license protects the job, not the ownership. Arizona now lets non-lawyers own law firms, and in 2025 KPMG became the first Big Four firm cleared to practice law in the U.S. The moat stays. The castle changes hands.</p><p><strong>Deed.</strong> Lasting value comes from ownership: rights, not fees (songwriters formed ASCAP in 1914 and have collected royalties ever since); owning the firm, since one professional with AI has the reach of a small firm; and accountability, because AI can't owe a fiduciary duty and someone must sign.</p><p>The Pullman porters were handed a job designed as a trap — low wages, tips, and a uniform that made every man answer to "George." They organized it into the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, won a landmark contract in 1937, and anchored a Black middle class. The machine will pay you to teach it. The question is whether you own anything it learned.</p><p><strong>Ownership helps people keep value. A bigger question is what humans are for when the machine does the work.
