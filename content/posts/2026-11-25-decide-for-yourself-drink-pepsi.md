<p>Nobody wants to believe they're being manipulated, least of all in America, where we're told every day how freely we think.</p><p>Modern persuasion rests on one insight: people resist being told what to do, but they will defend to the death what they believe they chose. In 1929, Edward Bernays, Sigmund Freud's nephew, was hired to sell cigarettes to women. He had young women light Lucky Strikes in New York's Easter Parade and told the press they were women's rights marchers lighting "torches of freedom." Smoking became liberation, and the women thought it was their idea.</p><p>That insight now runs the most profitable business on earth. Alphabet reported $402.8 billion in revenue for 2025, most of it from advertising. At Meta, advertising brought in $196 billion, more than 97% of total revenue. Global ad revenue is on pace to hit $1.3 trillion in 2026. Two of the richest companies in history sell a single product: your attention, and a chance to shape what you do with it.</p><p>Pepsi knows the method better than anyone. In 1947, Pepsi-Cola hired Edward Boyd to build a special markets team of African American salesmen. Boyd's ads showed black families as middle-class consumers instead of caricatures, and Pepsi's share in Chicago overtook Coke's for the first time. Then a 1947 memo raised concerns that white consumers had begun to see Pepsi as a "negro drink," and the team was dismantled. Seventy years later, Pepsi ran an ad in which a can of soda calmed a protest line, and pulled it within a day. The instinct hadn't changed: take what people feel most deeply and sell it back to them.</p><p>A trillion dollars a year doesn't buy information. It buys the feeling that the conclusion was yours. But this power is there for the people too: money talks. Stop feeding attention to the media that misleads you, and its revenue suffers. When revenue suffers, the media starts listening to you.</p><p><strong>Advertising is only one brick in the wall. The wall also trains us and rewrites what we remember.
