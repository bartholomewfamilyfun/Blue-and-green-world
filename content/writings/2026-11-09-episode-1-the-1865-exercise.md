  In 1865, four million freed people entered a country determined to give them nothing: no land, no capital, every high-status door closed. A century and a half later, their descendants are central to some of the world's largest industries in sports and entertainment. That wasn't a miracle. It was a mechanism, and it can be traced in eight steps — from jockeys dominating the first Kentucky Derby, to the backlash that pushed them out, to basketball in crowded northern cities, Negro League pipelines funded by unlikely partners, and one executive forcing MTV to play Michael Jackson. It happened largely by accident, and other branches of history ended in tragedy. The 1865 Exercise uses this path to examine today's frightening technologies: find the crack nobody's pricing, follow who captures the value, and argue with receipts that a fantastic future is also a realistic one.

<p>Every week brings another headline: AI is coming for your job, automation is coming for your industry, the machines are already faster at the thing you spent your whole life learning. A major shift is occurring in the labor market, and it's happening quickly.</p><p>But before we spend a full episode on automation — and we will — we need to install a piece of mental hardware first. The fear that “the machines are coming for us” isn't new. It's wearing a new outfit. The last time a whole class of people had zero leverage, zero capital, and every institutional door closed to them, the outcome 150 years later wasn't collapse. It became partnership in one of the largest industries on the planet.</p><p>That's not a feel-good story. It's a mechanism. And once you can see the mechanism, you don't just interrogate the future — you understand it well enough to build it. We don't have to hope we're living in the one universe that turns out fine. We can create it.</p><h2>Let's Start 150 Years Ago</h2><p>In 1865, following the Civil War, the United States emancipated enslaved peoples. Nobody standing at emancipation would have anticipated the NBA, dominance on the Olympic podium, or the birth of blues, jazz, rock and roll, pop, and hip-hop as an outcome. But the mechanism that produced those things, while remarkable, unfolded largely accidentally. We can improve upon it — if we're willing to embrace change intentionally.</p><p>That's our hypothesis. The best way to understand what an unrecognizable technology does to the world in 50 years isn't to stare at it. It's to find a moment in history where the outcome was already unimaginable to the people living it — and reverse-engineer exactly how it happened.</p><p>That's what 1865 gives us. It's not a road map. It's a grounding exercise — one that lets us extrapolate grand futures while keeping them from becoming impossible dreams. They become actual goals, with realistic steps rooted in the present. AI, gene editing, surveillance, machine sentience — we'll explore all of it.</p><h2>The 1865 Exercise</h2><h3>Step 1: Powerless selves at the precipice of the change mechanism</h3><p>In 1865, the United States freed four million enslaved people. Bodies became legally self-owned. For the first time, labor and its proceeds belonged to the person who performed it.</p><h3>Step 2: Dismal prospects</h3><p>Reconstructionist efforts that initially showed promise soon became protracted, unsexy negotiations. Somebody else's problem in a self-interested world. No land. Capital denied. Education restricted. Professions gatekept. Every high-status path blocked.</p><h3>Step 3: Opportunity presents</h3><p>Talent gets funneled into the few arenas where gatekeeping fails. Physical contest is one where the result can't be rigged. You can deny a loan quietly. You can't deny who crossed the finish line first in front of a paying crowd — and gamblers care about winning, not purity. Did you know thirteen of the fifteen jockeys in the first Kentucky Derby in 1875 were Black? Within a decade of emancipation, formerly enslaved men were the elite performers in America's premier sporting event.</p><h3>Step 4: The mechanism is perverted</h3><p>Black jockeys dominate, then get pushed out within a generation. Jack Johnson takes the heavyweight title in 1908 and triggers a national panic. The Mann Act — better known as the White Slave Act. Rhetoric from the highest levels of government and media, all designed to frighten and control the masses. Sounds familiar?</p><p>It should, because this is the path of technology we fear. The wheel of emancipation, promising equality, becomes a square block meant to crush Black people and serve as a foundation stone for someone else's plans.</p><p>Those on top want to limit the outcome of the mechanism. While advocating for change, they are just as frightened as the rest of us — trying to control technology; stockpiling resources in a few instead of reinvesting in Darwinian diversity; and promoting progress only as far as it serves and entrenches their position.</p><p>This is a real concern. This is the point where corruption dominates and empires fall. If life exists throughout the universe, it is a good bet that the perversion of progress is the cancer that makes advanced species apoptose. When finite beings make their limited span the barrier for time and technology's infinite reach.</p><p>But we live in an America that at least partially overcame this corruption. And by naming the demon, we give ourselves a better than accidental chance of passing this hurdle.</p><h3>Step 5: Concentration of resources</h3><p>The Great Migration builds density. Millions of Black Americans move into northern cities — concrete jungles, schoolyards, YMCAs. Basketball, invented 1891, needs almost no equipment or land. A near-zero-cost sport lands in the communities with the least capital.</p><h3>Step 6: Heroes arise</h3><p>Segregation forces parallel institutions. Negro Leagues, Black college athletics. Excluded from the main system, an entire scouting and development pipeline gets built outside the market. And yet, someone still had to fund it.</p><p>During segregation, Jewish businessmen play an outsized role bankrolling Negro League baseball — team owners like Eddie Gottlieb and Abe Saperstein provide real financial infrastructure the mainstream refuses to. Saperstein, of Harlem Globetrotters fame, later founds a pro basketball league of his own, the American Basketball League.</p><p>Jews — themselves a marginalized group clawing for their own foothold in America — step into a gap mainstream America refuses to fill. The partnership is transactional, complicated, and real. We remember the heroes who emerge from these pipelines: Jesse Owens, Dr. J, Jackie Robinson. But the ledger is incomplete without acknowledging who kept the lights on.</p><p>The greatness that follows could not have been written without one marginalized group choosing commerce over the color line — while the rest of America chose contempt.</p><h3>Step 7: Mechanisms feed mechanisms</h3><p>In the late 1970s, the music video grows in popularity and is poised to revolutionize how we consume music. At the forefront, MTV rises — an avant-garde cable station that sees the future and intends to lead it. But for all its forward thinking, MTV is essentially segregated.</p><p>Enter Walter Yetnikoff, the CBS Records president, who threatens to pull his label's entire roster if MTV won't air the video of one young Black man — Michael Jackson. He gets his way. The rest is pop music history.</p><h3>Step 8: The impossible future</h3><p>And just like that — a step-by-step, pragmatic path to an impossible dream.</p><p>From negro spirituals in a cotton field to Eleanor Roosevelt standing side by side with Marian Anderson at the Lincoln Memorial. From a runaway slave to Usain Bolt and LeBron James. The shackled become the sovereign.</p><p>The reason no one in 1865 could have predicted the present is not that they lacked imagination — it's that they refused to expand their horizons. It wasn't hard to imagine a physically gifted Black man. It was the scale of what came after that was unfathomable.</p><p>But 1865 until today is just one branch on a tree of outcomes. A tree with plenty of other branches, most of them tragic — where that same group gets ground down for another century and the world never hears a single name. Dalits of India, anyone?</p><p>Both outcomes are “logical.” But in America, one of them happened because of specific cracks, specific choices, specific people who accidentally made the impossible a reality.</p><p>That's the point of this series. We're looking for the cracks — intentionally, while the technology is still young enough that we control our destiny.</p><p>Every episode, we're going to approach a scary technology. Find the crack nobody's pricing. Follow who generates the value versus who captures it. Name what's being suppressed and manipulated. And argue, with receipts, that fantastic outcomes are also realistic outcomes.</p><p>This is Episode 1 of (we'll see 😉).</p><p>The 1865 Exercise — a series using history's biggest blind spot to connect with the technologies that scare us today. Episodes stand alone. Catch up anytime. 🚀</p><hr />
