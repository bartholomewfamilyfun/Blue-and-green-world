<h2>Life Isn't Fair</h2><p>Life isn't fair, and it never has been. Some children are born into bodies that work and to families that can afford to keep them working. Others are born owing a debt they never signed for.</p><p>Victoria Gray was three months old when her family learned which kind she was. Her grandmother was bathing her, and she would not stop crying. At the emergency room, doctors found her first sickle cell crisis. Hundreds more followed. It became the determining factor in her life. At any moment her own blood could clog a vessel, starve an organ or trigger a stroke.</p><p>In 2019 she volunteered to become the first person with sickle cell disease treated with CRISPR. Doctors removed stem cells from her bone marrow and edited a single gene. Then they infused more than two billion edited cells back into her body.</p><p>That is what Victoria Gray received. In late 2023 Casgevy became the first CRISPR medicine approved anywhere. In the trials, more than nine in ten patients went at least a year without a single pain crisis. For a disease defined by pain, that is a new life.</p><p>For all of history, the genetic lottery was the one unfairness nobody could appeal. Gene editing changes that.</p><h2>Why Us First?</h2><p>The first CRISPR cure didn't go to sickle cell because white people wanted to "try it out" on black people first.</p><p>But it wasn't a goodwill gesture either. Sickle cell went first because it was the easiest target in medicine. One well-understood defect causes it. The cells that need fixing live in the bone marrow and can be taken out, edited in a lab and put back. Cystic fibrosis affects the lungs and other organs that can't be taken out. Sickle cell was first because it was feasible now.</p><p>But let's not engage in gaslighting. Black Americans have a right to be pessimistic. Because they have been first in line before.</p><h3>Henrietta Lacks</h3><p>In 1951, a 31-year-old black mother of five named Henrietta Lacks was treated for cervical cancer at Johns Hopkins. Doctors took tissue from her tumor without her knowledge or consent. Her cells did something no human cells had done before. They kept dividing in the lab, indefinitely. The HeLa cell line became the workhorse of modern biology, used in developing the polio vaccine, in vitro fertilization, genetic mapping and the COVID-19 vaccines. Lacks died that year and was buried in an unmarked grave.</p><h3>The Tuskegee Experiment</h3><p>In 1932, the U.S. Public Health Service began a study of 600 black men in Macon County, Alabama, 399 of them with syphilis. They were told they were being treated for "bad blood," and in exchange they got free exams, meals and burial insurance. By 1943, penicillin was the standard cure. The men were never offered it. The study ran for forty years, until a whistleblower took it to the press in 1972.</p><h3>The History and Humanity You Missed</h3><p>Here's a detail that rarely makes the story. In those same years, at the same Tuskegee Institute, the Army was training the Tuskegee Airmen. These black pilots became one of the most celebrated units of World War II. One town, two stories. The most shameful study in American medicine, and some of the proudest service in American military history, side by side.</p><p>Which story do you know better? For many people, it's the syphilis study. A community lied to for generations has learned to expect the worst. The pendulum has swung so far that the betrayal is better remembered than the triumph. That's misunderstood history, and we'll come back to it.</p><aside class="pullquote">The worst of history built the guardrails for the best.</aside><p>For now, notice the difference between Henrietta Lacks and Victoria Gray. Lacks was never asked. Gray volunteered, signed a consent form and was told the risks. That difference didn't fall from the sky. The outrage over Tuskegee produced the National Research Act of 1974 and the modern system of informed consent and ethics review that governed Gray's trial. The worst of history built the guardrails for the best. That's progress.</p><h2>History Misunderstood</h2><p>Which brings us back to the pendulum. The worst lesson to take from Henrietta Lacks or Tuskegee is shame, the kind that makes people avoid history or remember it once a year in February as a wound. And no part of American history is more misunderstood, especially by the people it supposedly insults, than the three-fifths clause of the US Constitution.</p><p>Many Americans believe the Constitution declared black people three-fifths of a human being. It didn't. The clause decided how to count population for seats in Congress: "the whole Number of free Persons" plus "three fifths of all other Persons." Free black Americans were counted in full, exactly like free white Americans. Please understand what I just said. "Other persons" is not a euphemism for black people. Free black people were counted as full persons, just like free white people.</p><p>Here's a further twist. The slaveholders wanted enslaved people counted in full. That wasn't respect. More people meant more seats in Congress, more electoral votes and more power for the slaveholding South.</p><p>The three-fifths compromise was not a diminution of black people. Northern delegates would have preferred enslaved people not be counted at all, to stop slaveholders from turning human property into political power. The three-fifths compromise weakened the political power of the slave-owning South.</p><p>The proper lesson of history is almost never shame. It's interdependence. The sick feeling you get when you think about Tuskegee isn't a burden to carry. It's evidence that you already know what those doctors forgot. We are bound to each other, and harm to one is harm that affects us all in one way or another.</p><h3>Of Mice and Men</h3><p>That raises the question this episode is built around. Is gene editing here to level the playing field? Or will it leave some people mice, so that others can become relative men?</p><p>Society began as a clever way to protect and nurture its members. Somewhere along the way the arrow flipped. Society became the machine to be served, and people became its parts: the serf, the soldier, the field hand, the factory hand, each valued for output. Now, with perfect irony, we're building actual machines to replace the "lazy" humans who never quite managed to behave like machines. As we noted in Episode 3 (shameless plug), that's why automation scares us. We still think like a duty-based society, afraid the robots are coming for our serfdom.</p><p><strong>Gene editing poses the same fork, only deeper. This time the question isn't who does the work. It's who gets to be called human</strong>.</p><h2>Relative Monkeys</h2><p>If we don't learn from history:</p><p>It starts with cures priced like mansions.</p><p>Oh, did I mention: the sickle cell cure costs about $2.2 million. That's one reason only a few hundred of the 100,000 people afflicted with the disease have received it so far.</p><p>Then it moves from treatment to upgrades: denser bones, sharper memory, longer lives, sold first to those who can pay. Edits made to embryos pass to their children. A few generations later, the haves have engineered themselves into something new.</p><p>The have-nots are left behind as the species' earlier draft, relative monkeys, in both senses. <strong>Darwin already told us we're related. The fear is that the rest of us become the relatives.</strong></p><aside class="pullquote">A $2.2 million therapy is a lottery ticket. An inherited upgrade is a dynasty.</aside><p>This isn't science fiction. In 2018, a Chinese scientist named He Jiankui announced the birth of twin girls whose embryos he had edited, and he was sent to prison. The world condemned it, rightly. But the first cure's price shows how upgrades could be handed out if we're not vigilant to learn from the past and insist that humans have humanity. A $2.2 million therapy is a lottery ticket. An inherited upgrade is a dynasty. Both belong to a dystopian future we can choose right now to alter.</p><h2>Beyond</h2><p>There's evidence that we can, because decency on a global scale has happened before. Smallpox, which killed hundreds of millions of people, was eradicated in 1980. Wild polio has been cut by more than 99 percent. PEPFAR, launched in 2003, is credited with saving 26 million lives and letting 7.8 million babies be born without HIV. It's an American program aimed mostly at people who could never pay.</p><p>But decency can also be reversed. In 2025, after U.S. aid was cut and reorganized, about two million fewer people received PEPFAR-supported treatment, though the government disputes the toll. So the receipt cuts both ways. What humanity has done, it can do again, but it has to keep choosing to do so.</p><h3>Humans with Humanity</h3><p>If we do learn from history:</p><p>The edits that already exist point to a future that sounds like fiction. Baby KJ Muldoon was born with a rare and often fatal liver disorder. In 2025, he received a CRISPR treatment designed for him alone, and he is thriving. The FDA is now building a pathway for exactly these one-patient cures. Sickle cell, thalassemia, cystic fibrosis. Diseases that have stalked families for generations are starting to look like solvable problems. A healthy birth stops being luck.</p><p>Then there's the question that sounds like science fiction until you look at the data. Could gene editing help us leave the planet? Our bodies are built for Earth. In microgravity, astronauts lose 1 to 1.5 percent of their bone mass a month, roughly what an aging adult on Earth loses in a year. A trip to Mars takes almost a year each way. That means on a trip to Mars a person's bones could age decades. The limits of space exploration may be our bodies, not our rockets.</p><h3>Mice in Space?</h3><p>But the solution to even this problem may already be in our collective genes. A rare variant of a gene called LRP5 gives some people unusually dense, fracture-resistant bones, partly because it resists sclerostin, a protein the body makes that puts the brakes on bone growth. In 2011, NASA sent mice to space to study that same protein. The mice were given an antibody to see whether blocking sclerostin could prevent microgravity-induced bone loss. Those results helped support FDA approval for romosozumab, a sclerostin blocker that treats osteoporosis. Tardigrades, microscopic animals that survive radiation that would kill us, make a protein called Dsup that shields DNA. Researchers have delivered it into mice. This research could lead to innovations that one day protect humans from the ionizing radiation of space. All of this is early. But the possibilities are real. Humans able to live on the Moon, Mars, or a changing Earth.</p><h3>Gene Editing: The Evolutionary Fork</h3><p>Down one path, we don't learn from history, and a select few rewrite themselves and start believing they're gods.</p><p>Down a better path, we deliver a collective promise where every child starts with a working body.</p><p>Which road we take depends on what we learn from those who went first. Henrietta Lacks's cells helped build vaccines that have protected billions. The Tuskegee study produced the rules that protected Victoria Gray. Victoria Gray's courage opened the door for everyone who follows. None of them were cut off from the rest of us. Their stories are ours.</p><p>History properly learned doesn't leave us with guilt and hopelessness. It leaves us knowing that our fates are joined, and that a cure for one becomes a cure for all only if we decide it should. Read that way, gene editing isn't the end of being human. It may be the chance to put more humanity into humans than ever before.</p><p>With history properly learned, gene editing takes us beyond.</p><p>This is Episode 4 of (we'll see 😉).</p><p>The 1865 Exercise — a series using history's biggest blind spot to connect with the technologies that scare us today.
