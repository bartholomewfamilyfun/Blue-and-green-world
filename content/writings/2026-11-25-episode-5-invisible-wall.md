<h2>The Wall You Can't See</h2><p>The Berlin Wall was a cruel structure, but it was an honest one. You could touch it. You knew which side you were on, who had built it, and where it ended. Everyone behind it knew they were oppressed and knew exactly what to push against.</p><p>Americans are told we live in the freest country on earth, and in some ways it's true. But there is a wall in America too. It's made of glass, and most people will live their whole lives without noticing it. They feel the pressure, look up at a blue sky, and decide they must be imagining things. Even someone who sees through it has no idea where to push, because nobody can say where the wall begins or ends.</p><p>On a simple level, the invisible wall is the set of barriers around our information: what platforms allow, what news gets funded, what gets remembered. On a deeper level, it's the manipulation of our attention, which makes us believe that we are choosing what we think. The first kind of wall controls what you can say. The second controls what you want to say.</p><h2>The Wall Is Legal</h2><p>Try posting a comment the platform doesn't want, and you'll find out quickly that the free exchange of ideas has terms of service. I watched this happen on YouTube during the Sean Combs scandal. For two years, videos and comments mocked him with homophobic slurs, and creators boosted their careers with bits questioning his sexuality, on a platform whose rules prohibit exactly that. When I posted a comment defending him as a human being (not his crimes, his humanity), it wouldn't post. But the insults did.</p><p>Ask Google, which owns YouTube, and it will build you an airtight case that YouTube is a private company free to host what it pleases. The courts agree. In 2020, the Ninth Circuit ruled that despite YouTube's ubiquity and its role as a public-facing platform, it remains a private forum, not subject to the First Amendment.</p><p>The wall shapes our attention as much as speech. In January 2025, the United States formally determined that the Rapid Support Forces had committed genocide in Sudan. You'd hardly know it from your feed. Some crises saturate every screen while others barely trend, and nobody voted on which is which. The wall doesn't create any physical boundaries. It hijacks the mind, so physical impediment is unnecessary.</p><h2>Sesame Street Is Our Public News</h2><p>I first noticed the wall in 2003, while studying abroad in Sweden. The country had multiple publicly owned news stations, funded by citizens and answerable to them. Back home, the most notable public television network was PBS, with its flagship program: <em>Sesame Street</em>.</p><p>Since then, billionaires have bought newspapers: Jeff Bezos the <em>Washington Post</em> in 2013, Patrick Soon-Shiong the <em>Los Angeles Times</em> in 2018. In July 2025, Congress cut $1.1 billion in public broadcasting funding, and the Corporation for Public Broadcasting, created in 1967 to give Americans news that answered to the public, began shutting down.</p><p>What's left is news that answers to owners and advertisers. That doesn't make every story false. It makes every story a product, built for the people who pay for it. In American media, the people who pay are mostly not the audience.</p><h2>Decide for Yourself: Drink Pepsi</h2><blockquote><p><em>Nobody wants to believe they're being manipulated, least of all in America, where we're told every day how freely we think.</em></p></blockquote><p>Modern persuasion rests on one insight: people resist being told what to do, but they will defend to the death what they believe they chose. In 1929, Edward Bernays, Sigmund Freud's nephew, was hired to sell cigarettes to women. He had young women light Lucky Strikes in New York's Easter Parade and told the press they were women's rights marchers lighting "torches of freedom." Smoking became liberation, and the women thought it was their idea.</p><p>That insight now runs the most profitable business on earth. Alphabet reported $402.8 billion in revenue for 2025, most of it from advertising. At Meta, advertising brought in $196 billion, more than 97% of total revenue. Global ad revenue is on pace to hit $1.3 trillion in 2026, its highest share of GDP since 1999. Two of the richest companies in history sell a single product: your attention, and a chance to shape what you do with it.</p><p>Pepsi knows the method better than anyone. In 1947, Pepsi-Cola hired Edward Boyd to build a special markets team of African American salesmen. Boyd's ads showed black families as middle-class consumers instead of caricatures, and Pepsi's share in Chicago overtook Coke's for the first time. Then a 1947 memo raised concerns that white consumers had begun to see Pepsi as a "negro drink," and the team was dismantled.</p><p>Nearly ninety years after Bernays, Pepsi ran an ad in which a can of soda calmed a protest line, and pulled it within a day. The instinct hadn't changed: take what people feel most deeply and sell it back to them.</p><p>A trillion dollars a year doesn't buy information. It buys the feeling that the conclusion was yours. But this power is there for the people too: money talks. Stop feeding attention to the media that misleads you, and its revenue suffers. When revenue suffers, the media starts listening to you.</p><h2>The Conditioning Chamber</h2><p>People call social media an echo chamber, and the phrase is too gentle. An echo chamber only repeats you. This chamber punishes some speech, rewards other speech, and feeds you whatever keeps you agitated. Do that long enough and it doesn't reinforce your opinion; it installs one. It's a Pavlovian conditioning chamber.</p><p>The real test of mind control isn't whether you can make people believe something. It's whether, after they believe it, you can make them do the opposite on command, without anyone noticing the switch. Orwell wrote that scene. During Hate Week in <em>1984</em>, a speaker denouncing the enemy is handed a note, and mid-sentence the enemy changes. The crowd turns its fury on the new target, and nobody admits the old one was ever different.</p><aside class="pullquote">A rule that can be suspended on cue was never a principle; it was a setting.</aside><p>The Sean Combs case worked as that kind of test. For a decade we have been taught, correctly, that mocking a person's sexuality is cruel. Then the wall marked one man as rotten meat, and the same people who had learned the rule broke it with enthusiasm, without penalty, and without noticing the contradiction. A rule that can be suspended on cue was never a principle; it was a setting.</p><h2>From the Ministry of Truth to Wikipedia</h2><p>In <em>1984</em>, the Ministry of Truth controlled the future by controlling the past. Whenever the story changed, clerks rewrote the old newspapers and books to match. Memories fade, witnesses die, and what children read in school becomes what happened.</p><p>Americans treat that as dystopian fiction. But it's American history. After Reconstruction, Confederate memorial groups rewrote the war in the nation's schoolbooks: the freedmen became corrupt, Reconstruction a disaster, and the Klan redeemers. By 1940, that version dominated classrooms nationwide. As late as 1961, an Alabama textbook called slavery the earliest form of social security in the United States.</p><p>But Americans took the truth back. Black historians challenged the myth as early as 1913, and black parents and grandparents corrected the lessons their children brought home. In 1926, the historian Carter G. Woodson launched Negro History Week so the real record would be taught, not whispered. It grew into Black History Month. W.E.B. Du Bois published <em>Black Reconstruction</em> in 1935. After 1950, historians took the myth apart piece by piece. When Mississippi refused to approve an honest state history textbook, its authors sued and won, and in 1980 a federal judge ordered the state to approve it. It's proof that an entire system of lies can be corrected by decent Americans who refuse to forget.</p><p>But for all George Orwell's prescience, what he could not foresee was Wikipedia. Its administrators can delete pages and hide individual revisions from public view. A handful of Wikipedians can undo history with the click of a button. Unlike the post-Reconstruction textbooks, which could be answered with honestly written books, Wikipedia is part of the wall. You can present a true version if you like. But as long as people allow their attention to be directed by the wall, your perspective will remain invisible.</p><p>That's the price of a wall no one can see. People who can't tell what's true stop trusting anything. In 1958, 73% of Americans trusted the federal government to do the right thing most of the time; by 2024, 22%. We absorb more media than any generation in history, and we're more confused and more anxious for it.</p><h2>The Blue and Green World</h2><aside class="pullquote">Reclaiming our sense of well-being is going to require us to move beyond the wall.</aside><p>Reclaiming our sense of well-being is going to require us to move beyond the wall.</p><p>Medicine has been here before. At the start of the pharmaceutical age, a pill for every ill was supposed to be the destination of health science. Fifty years later, the most durable gains come from lifestyle medicine: movement, sleep, food, connection. People learned the limits of the cure and discovered that living well delivers something no prescription can: confidence that their health belongs to them.</p><p>The internet's greatest innovation may be the same discovery. Some of the most informative content on YouTube involves ordinary people traveling to countries the headlines warn them away from, to see for themselves. The revolution will not be televised. It will be walked into, one firsthand experience at a time.</p><p>You cannot process the volume of information aimed at you every day, and you were never meant to. Notice what's in front of you: your neighbors, your town, the people you can call by name. The anxiety that comes from sensory overload doesn't survive an ordinary afternoon outdoors.</p><p>If you hold a strong conviction on a hot-button topic, ask honestly where you got it. Then check it against the world. Not the world on your screen. The blue and green one.</p><p>This is Episode 5 of (we'll see 😉).</p><p>The 1865 Exercise — a series using history's biggest blind spot to connect with the technologies that scare us today.
