<p>Everyone is arguing about whether to allow data centers in our communities. Almost no one is arguing about what to ask for in exchange.</p><p>In Richland Parish, Louisiana, teachers opened their pay statements this year and found bonuses of nearly $51,000. The year before, the same bonus had been about $10,200. The money came from a surge in local sales tax collections tied to construction of Meta's Hyperion data center, a $27 billion project rising out of farmland in the state.</p><p>That is what's really good. It is also the most misleading number in the entire American data center debate.</p><p>The bonus was funded by construction-phase sales tax — a revenue stream that only exists during construction. Parish officials have been straightforward about this: it's likely to last three to five years. After that, the data center receives a thirty-year property tax abatement. The community windfall is short-term. The data center tax break is long-term.</p><p>This pattern — a large visible benefit arriving first, the durable terms sitting underneath — leaves many people troubled. But most can't quite put their finger on WHY. So they raise objections about electricity costs, water usage, noise, and a concern that politicians are putting personal interests above the public good. All concerns you can hear about in depth elsewhere.</p><p>What I want to discuss are the opportunities that partnering with data centers can provide to our communities. Because a claim that our politicians are selling us out is only meaningful if we know what a good deal looks like.</p><h2>Section One: The Global E-Waste Trade</h2><h3>The Comparison</h3><p>A productive gold mine processes rock containing somewhere between one and five grams of gold per tonne. To get it out, you remove a hillside, crush it, treat it with cyanide, and leave behind rubble someone will be managing for a century.</p><p>A tonne of high-grade circuit boards contains between 150 and 400 grams of gold.</p><p>Sit with that for a moment.</p><p>Once you understand where the actual value lies, the entire conversation about data centers turns on its head.</p><h3>The mine is already open</h3><p>We think of a retired server as garbage with some salvage value. It is more accurate to say it is ore of exceptional grade that has already been mined, refined, concentrated, delivered to a known address, catalogued by serial number, and stacked in rows under a roof with the power already run to it. Mixed electronic waste averages roughly sixty times the gold concentration of natural ore. The United Nations put the metal value embedded in a single year of global e-waste at about $91 billion, of which something like $28 billion is actually recovered. The rest is left in the ground, so to speak. Except the ground is a warehouse, and we built it on purpose.</p><p>Hyperscale buildings last fifteen to twenty years. But the hardware inside does not. AI hardware becomes obsolete in roughly eighteen to thirty-six months — not broken, just competitively outpaced, which in this industry amounts to the same thing. So the building doesn't produce one windfall of ore at the end of its life. It produces a fresh seam every two years, five to ten times over, starting almost immediately.</p><p>The first big one is arriving now. Industry analysts expect the first real wave of GPU-dense AI servers to enter decommissioning between 2026 and 2029 — the hardware bought during the 2022–2024 buildout. The global data center decommissioning market has already reached $12.95 billion — growth driven almost entirely by AI turnover, not by facilities closing. The window is open now.</p><h3>The Important Question</h3><p>Take Loudoun County, Virginia — Data Center Alley, the densest concentration of compute on Earth. Roughly 233 data center buildings covering about 50 million square feet, up from 20 million in 2019. Northern Virginia carries close to 5,000 megawatts of commissioned capacity, more than twice Beijing, its nearest competitor. An estimated 70 percent of global internet traffic passes through daily. Global super hub of internet traffic. Global super hub of unrefined rare earth ore. Right here in the USA.</p><p>Now who owns the servers and the ore inside them? Obviously blue-chip mega-tech companies. But the important question is: who doesn't own them — and who isn't even asking for a stake in the ongoing windfall of server turnover?</p><p>Loudoun County. The State of Virginia. The people of Virginia. Not to mention the rest of us, around the country, engaged in similar negotiations — most of us, until now, clueless about what we should actually be negotiating for. You're welcome.</p><h3>Sixty-One Bills</h3><p>But Virginia is not a closed case. The negotiation is happening right now, in public, and what's on the table is more instructive than any argument I could make. Since 2010, Virginia has exempted data center computer equipment from retail sales and use tax. In fiscal 2024 alone, that exemption was worth about $1 billion, up from $685 million the year before. Virginia's own legislative review agency called it the largest economic development incentive the state supports, and found that five companies received 82 percent of the benefit in a single year.</p><p>The state deliberately waived tax on the hardware. The hardware is the ore. That is not a scandal — the exemption was a legitimate tool used to attract an industry, and it worked. But it is a decision, made in writing, about the most valuable transferable thing in the building. Which raises the obvious question of what else could have been written. The 2026 General Assembly session considered sixty-one data center bills: electricity taxes, noise standards, water regulation, reporting requirements. Sixty-one bills, and not one is about what happens to the metal.</p><p>To be fair, Virginia has suggested compensation for extending the hardware exemption past 2035 — $35 billion in new investment and 1,000 jobs to reach 2040, $100 billion to reach 2050. Those terms may be decent remuneration. But when the underlying value of the server components isn't being named, skepticism is warranted. Two industries: one providing compute services, one processing the most strategically contested materials on earth. Most people who consider themselves knowledgeable about data centers only know about one.</p><h3>Time to Start Thinking Forward</h3><p>Everything above is about who captures value. This part is about who does the work. This is where the history stops being an analogy and starts being a warning.</p><p>Extraction industries have a consistent structure. Somebody owns the deposit. Somebody does the physical labor of separating the valuable from the worthless. Somebody captures the margin. These have almost never been the same party.</p><h3>Global E-Waste Trade</h3><p>The global e-waste trade is a nearly perfect specimen. Under a quarter of the world's electronic waste is formally collected. Most of the rest is stripped by hand in low-income regions — open cable burning, acid baths — before the valuable fractions get shipped back to wealthy countries for refining. One study found residents near a Chinese processing site were 60 percent more likely to develop lung cancer than their neighbors thirty miles away. The value is separated by hand in one place and captured in another, and the health cost stays with the hands.</p><p>But that is not the only way to do it, and the alternative is being built right now. The recovery industry taking shape in the United States is a chemical process industry, not a scrapyard. USA Rare Earth's hydrometallurgical facility in Wheat Ridge, Colorado produced commercial-grade dysprosium and neodymium-praseodymium oxides from recycled magnet scrap this year. Their planned metal-making and magnet plant in Blacksburg, South Carolina is expected to create about 490 high-skill, high-wage jobs. Cyclic Materials announced an $82 million campus in McBee, South Carolina. These are refinery-grade operations: process chemists, operators, technicians, hazards managed the way a chemical plant manages them.</p><p>So the same material supports two completely different industries. One poisons a village. The other pays 490 people well to run a plant. Which one happens is not a technical question. It is a question of where the material is allowed to go, and who wrote that down.</p><h3>Three Card Monte</h3><p>Here is the part that should worry anyone in Loudoun County. The refining capacity is not being built in Virginia. Blacksburg, McBee, and a magnet plant in Sumter are all in South Carolina — a state with a small fraction of Virginia's compute. The material does not stay where the data centers are. It goes wherever the refiner is. Which means hosting the densest concentration of compute on Earth confers no advantage whatsoever in the industry that grows out of it, unless somebody deliberately arranges otherwise. The deposit is in one state, the value captured in another. Nobody did anything wrong — one state started building and the other didn't notice there was anything to build.</p><p>There is also a national dimension. China dominates rare earth refining and has used export controls as leverage — which is why domestic producers now advertise being among the only Western operations that can refine heavy rare earths at commercial quality at all. In that frame, recovering material from retired servers isn't an environmental story. It's a sovereignty story — and the feedstock is sitting in American buildings, on a two-year cycle, largely unclaimed.</p><h3>The Clause Nobody Wrote</h3><p>In 1865, four million people were freed into a country that denied them land and capital by design. They were not at the table. No room, no counsel, no vote, no legislature that answered to them. The asset was denied, and the value they generated was captured by others for a century.</p><p>Loudoun County has everything they were denied. A legislature. Lawyers. A research agency that produced a full study. Sixty-one bills in a single session. Hearings, a governor, an electorate. And the asset is not being denied. It is being left unrequested.</p><p>That is a different failure, and the difference is the entire reason to write any of this down. One was oppression, and it took a century and a movement to begin correcting. The other is a gap in imagination, closeable by one person putting one sentence into one permit before the trucks arrive. The first wave of AI hardware is being decommissioned between now and 2029. The material is already moving — to South Carolina, or out of the country entirely — and nobody has written a single clause about where it goes, who processes it, or who keeps the margin. The metal is coming out either way. The only question is whether anyone asked for it while the trucks were still being loaded.</p><h2>Section Two: Thermal Republic</h2><h3>Every data center is a furnace</h3><p>Every watt of electricity that enters a data center leaves as heat. Not most of it — effectively all of it. Computation is not a consumer of energy so much as a converter: electricity in, arithmetic performed, heat out. A hundred-megawatt facility is, thermodynamically speaking, a hundred-megawatt furnace that happens to also answer your email. The only question is whether that heat warms your community or warms the sky.</p><p>In most of the United States, it warms the sky — removed as cheaply as possible so the servers don't cook themselves, while billions of dollars of thermal energy are abandoned in a country that burns natural gas to heat its buildings.</p><h3>Why Finland can and most places can't</h3><p>Finland decided to run the arithmetic differently. Outside Helsinki in Espoo, Microsoft and Fortum have built the largest data center heat recovery system in the world. It's now running. The mechanism is ordinary: water leaves the servers at about 30°C — lukewarm, useless on its own. Heat pumps raise it to 86°C, hot enough to push into the city's district heating network. The scale is not. Roughly 250,000 people in greater Helsinki are connected to that network, and recovered server heat is expected to supply about 40 percent of their total annual heating demand. The projected emissions reduction is 400,000 tonnes of CO2 a year — two to three percent of Finland's entire national reduction target, from one installation.</p><p>It's not a one-off. Microsoft is doing a smaller version outside Copenhagen, and Meta has been heating homes in Odense with server exhaust since 2020. But two specific conditions had to be in place first, and neither is easy to reproduce.</p><h3>Cold air</h3><p>The largest overhead cost in running a data center is getting rid of heat. The industry measures overall efficiency with PUE — Power Usage Effectiveness — the ratio of total facility power to the power actually reaching the servers. Microsoft's Nordic sites run about 1.16, meaning roughly 16 watts of overhead for every 100 watts of computing. The industry average is closer to 1.5, meaning a third of all energy input goes to overhead. (Annual averages — worse in summer, when chillers run.) In a cold climate, a facility can dump heat straight into the outside air most of the year instead of running energy-hungry mechanical chillers, and it doesn't need water for cooling either. That efficiency is mostly a gift from the weather. This is why you can't simply plant mega-facilities in the middle of a desert — data centers require cooling from either an energy source or a water supply.</p><h3>Pipes That Already Existed</h3><p>What explains Chicago and New York, then, if cold air is the answer? Espoo has a district heating system, built in 1954: nine hundred kilometres of underground pipe, a quarter of a million customers, heat exchangers already in every basement. Central heating, but for an entire city. Microsoft did not build a heating system. They plugged into a seventy-year-old one and merely displaced the fuel. Finland is not generating energy. It is recovering value from energy already purchased — the elimination of a duplicate. Before, a Finnish town bought electricity to run computers and separately burned gas to heat buildings. Now it buys the electricity once and gets both. What disappears is the gas boiler.</p><h3>This Is How Industries Are Normally Born</h3><p>Building an industry on a byproduct is one of the most reliable patterns in industrial history. Early refineries wanted kerosene, for lamps. The light volatile fraction that came off alongside it was dangerous, hard to store, and commercially worthless — until the internal combustion engine arrived and turned the waste stream into the product that drove the twentieth century. Glycerin, left over from soap manufacturing, became dynamite. Natural gas was a waste product from oil wells. The shape is always the same: a byproduct is discarded because no infrastructure exists to move it and no buyer exists to receive it. Then one or both appear, and the accounting inverts. So why isn't this happening in America?</p><h3>The Sleeping Giant</h3><p>In the U.S. we are arguing over noise pollution while the rest of the world heats its countries with data center exhaust. Is there any district heating here at all? Turns out there is — and it's the largest in the world. Con Edison's steam network under Manhattan has run continuously since 1882, covers more than a hundred miles of underground pipe, and serves over 1,500 buildings — including the Empire State Building, Grand Central Terminal, Rockefeller Center, and the United Nations. It is larger than the next nine systems in the world combined.</p><p>So why isn't a data center heating Manhattan? Because Con Edison delivers steam at 450–475 degrees Fahrenheit. Water leaves a server at 86°F. After heat pumps do everything they can, it reaches about 187°F. You cannot feed a 450-degree steam system with 187-degree water. It's the wrong pipes. America built its district heating in the age of coal and skyscrapers; the Nordics built theirs in the age of efficiency.</p><p>There is no American version of Finland. We could structure future housing development around district heating with data centers in mind — but that limits us to cold environments, roughly half the country, and even then we're structuring entire deals for a benefit that only materializes in winter.</p><h3>American Mindset</h3><p>Shift the focus from residential to industrial, and a bouquet of opportunities opens up. A greenhouse wants heat in July. So does a fish farm. Food dehydration, pharmaceutical drying, chemical processing, industrial laundry — numerous industries need heat year-round. Finland's win was retrofitting an old network to warm existing homes. The American version is a synergistic industrial park where one tenant's biggest overhead is getting rid of heat and the other tenants can't get enough.</p><h3>A Call to Arms</h3><p>This is an opportunity that must be negotiated today. A heat connection is cheap while the trenches are already open for power and fiber. It is nearly impossible to add once the site is finished, the easements are settled, and the operator has no reason to negotiate. Every county in America currently reviewing a data center proposal is having a conversation about tax revenue and noise ordinances. Almost none of them are asking for a pipe. The heat is going to be produced either way. The only question is whether it heats the sky or fuels your economy.</p><h2>Section Three: Compute as an Asset</h2><h3>The Resource Curse</h3><p>There is a pattern that repeats so reliably across history that economists gave it a name, and the name is unusually blunt: the resource curse. A place receives something enormously valuable that it did not build. Oil under a desert nobody wanted. Minerals under a jungle. Diamonds, copper, gas. The windfall arrives, and the arrival feels like the end of the story — the reversal of fortune, the poor made rich. It is not the end of the story. It is the beginning of a second and much harder contest, and most places lose it.</p><p>The pattern has a shape. Money arrives suddenly, from sophisticated counterparties, to a party negotiating at the moment of least knowledge. Terms get set once. And then — this is the part everyone misses — the recipient has to keep it, which turns out to be a completely separate contest from winning it.</p><p>Norway found oil in 1969. It negotiated well, then built an institution to hold the proceeds: invest abroad, spend only the returns, never the principal. That fund is now worth roughly $2.3 trillion across some 7,200 companies — about $385,000 per citizen.</p><p>Nigeria also negotiated well. Its state oil company holds majority equity in its joint ventures — 55 to 60 percent — and its 2021 petroleum law requires operators to fund host community trusts. On paper, better terms than any American county has ever gotten from a data center. Nigeria even built the same savings mechanism: an Excess Crude Account, created in 2004 to bank surplus oil revenue. By 2007 it held $20 billion. By 2012, $3.5 billion. By 2015, $2.1 billion. By June 2022: $376,655.</p><p>Same windfall. Same instrument. Nigeria owned the asset and still lost it. Which is the whole lesson. It's not how much you make, it's how much you keep.</p><p>Now let's take that into the data center negotiations currently in progress — not to judge, but to work together to figure out how we can collectively own a piece of this technological windfall rather than simply receive fungible remuneration.</p><h3>Current Negotiations</h3><p>Back to the most successful data center hub in the world: Data Center Alley in Virginia. In Loudoun County, data centers generate 38 percent of the county's general fund — over a third of everything Loudoun spends — on the order of $1.1 billion a year. That money has allowed the county to cut its residential property tax rate every single year for a decade.</p><p>Now watch what Loudoun did next. In 2023, the Board of Supervisors created a Revenue Stabilization Fund — nearly $120 million by the 2026 budget — to absorb the gap between budgeted and actual data center revenue. The most successful data center county in the world built a shock absorber against its own success. Why? Because the board knows it's holding a revenue stream it does not control, from an industry it cannot compel to stay, assessed on equipment that depreciates fast and can be loaded onto trucks.</p><p>Remember Richland Parish, Louisiana, where a data center deal enabled a one-time $51,000 teacher bonus? Their officials accurately describe that windfall as short-term, tied to a construction phase lasting three to five years — after which a thirty-year property tax abatement takes over.</p><p>Cash payments are a claim on somebody else's continued decision to pay. They lack ownership. They lack a say in the decision-making process.</p><h3>Compute as an Asset</h3><p>Here is the alternative, and almost nobody is negotiating for it. A data center's product is not tax revenue. Tax revenue is a side effect. The product is computational capacity — and unlike revenue, capacity is a physical thing that exists inside the building, inside the county line, for as long as the machines run.</p><p>The deal could be structured so that a defined share of this facility's capacity belongs to this county, permanently, as a condition of the permit. Two percent. One percent. The number matters less than the category. Because cash and capacity behave in opposite ways. Cash is consumed in the fiscal year it arrives. Capacity is held. It sits in a building on a road in the county, and it does not evaporate when the market turns or the exemption expires. One is income. The other is an asset. I forgive you if your mind isn't immediately blown by this suggestion. I mean, what would a rural parish do with a supercomputer? Nobody there knows how to use it. There is no staff, no expertise, no obvious application. The county would be taking possession of something it cannot operate, in exchange for money it can definitely use. Put that way, it sounds less like a negotiating win than a negotiating error.</p><h3>The Value of Ownership</h3><p>It's time we go back to the 1865 exercise and talk about the value of ownership. Michael Jordan is the greatest basketball player who ever lived. He made about $94 million in salary across his entire playing career. In 2010 he bought the Charlotte Bobcats at a $275 million valuation. Thirteen years later, he sold at a $3 billion valuation. He was not a good owner. Didn't matter. A 10x return. That's the power of ownership. PERIOD.</p><p>But let's find an even better analogy. By the 1930s, private utilities had concluded that rural America was not worth electrifying. Too few customers, too many miles of line, no return. Roughly nine in ten American farms had no electricity, and the market had determined they never should. What would a farmer do with electricity? Run a milking machine. Pump water. Read after dark. Modest things — not obviously worth stringing copper across thousands of empty miles.</p><p>The Rural Electrification Administration was created in 1936 and financed cooperatives to build the lines anyway. The measurable short-run returns were agricultural — higher crop output, better productivity, land values that held when they were falling everywhere else. Respectable, unglamorous, easy to undersell. The part nobody forecast was the ownership. Those cooperatives were locally held, and they still are. Today they serve over 42 million people across 47 states, maintain 42 percent of America's distribution lines, and deliver 11 percent of the nation's electricity.</p><p>At the negotiating table, big industry assumed that investing in the latest technology for regular folks would not be productive. But America's greatness ultimately came through ownership, wealth, and brain trust for all. Even Blacks who were denied every institutional opportunity produced landowners, entrepreneurs, founded universities, and built a culture the entire world imports. Unrivaled by their counterparts worldwide. This is an opportunity to continue that tradition. Community ownership of supercompute can be a transformational technology that advances this country in ways we cannot yet imagine. And even setting aside grand futures, compute is an appreciable asset that will outperform cash over time. That alone is undeniable.</p><h3>Takeaway</h3><p>The time to negotiate the future of data centers is now. If our conversations stop at noise levels and electricity bills, our government is failing us — and we're failing our children. Whether you're drawn to the billions in rare earth materials on server boards, the symbiotic use of data center heat to drive American industry, or supercompute as an appreciable asset — we should be embracing data centers in some form. Our country has a bright future if we take the time to understand what's available and seize it.</p><p>This is Episode 2 of (we'll see 😉).</p><p>The 1865 Exercise — a series using history's biggest blind spot to connect with the technologies that scare us today.
