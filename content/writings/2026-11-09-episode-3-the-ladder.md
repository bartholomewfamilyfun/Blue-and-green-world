<h2>The Ladder</h2><p>For a century, the first years of a lawyer's career looked like drudgery. Document review. First drafts. Research memos no partner would read past page two. Firms billed it, associates resented it, and everyone understood it as a toll paid on the way to the real work.</p><p>It was never a toll. It was tuition. The thousand-page review was where a young lawyer learned what a smoking gun looks like before anyone pointed one out. The bad first draft was how she learned what a good one sounds like. The judgment that makes a senior partner worth his rate was assembled one tedious hour at a time, at the bottom of the ladder.</p><p>That bottom rung is being removed. Lateral associates — lawyers trained somewhere else, at someone else's expense — now make up 51% of associate hires at the country's 200 largest firms. Most big firms plan to grow their associate ranks through 2027, but only about a third plan to grow their first-year classes. They still want experienced lawyers. They have simply stopped wanting to make them.</p><p>Law is the clearest case, not the only one. The same pattern runs through finance, consulting, software, and marketing, and it raises a question no one in those industries has answered: if no one climbs the bottom of the ladder, where does the top come from?</p><h2>Codified vs. Tacit</h2><p>The best evidence comes from payroll data. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, working from millions of ADP records, found that employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is now roughly 19% below where it would be had it tracked their peers in less-exposed jobs. Experienced workers in those same occupations show no comparable decline.</p><p>The researchers' explanation is the key to this episode. AI substitutes for codified knowledge — the kind written in textbooks and manuals, the kind young workers arrive with. It does not yet substitute for tacit knowledge — the judgment built through years of doing. The junior analyst and the senior analyst hold the same title. The machine can replace only one of them.</p><p>Honesty requires the counter-evidence. Unemployment among recent college graduates in summer 2026 sat within the range of the previous four years. Plenty of "AI layoffs" are ordinary cost-cutting in a fashionable costume, and Forrester expects half of them to be quietly reversed. The displacement is real, but it isn't a flood. It's a drain: fewer people entering, not more people leaving. Drains are quieter than floods, and easier to ignore until the tank is empty.</p><h2>The Apprentice Clause</h2><p>In 1865, one of the first things several Southern legislatures did with freedom was regulate the ladder. Mississippi's Black Code included an apprenticeship law allowing courts to bind Black minors to masters — with preference given to their former owners. Training was the instrument of recapture. Whoever controlled the ladder controlled the class.</p><p>The jockeys tell the same story from the other direction. Thirteen of the fifteen riders in the first Kentucky Derby were Black. For a generation, Black jockeys held the deepest expertise in the sport. Then racing grew lucrative, and the expertise was reassigned. Within a generation they were gone from the Derby. Nothing about their skill had changed. What changed was who the skill was allowed to belong to.</p><p>Today's version is gentler, but structurally familiar. The models now replacing junior professionals were trained on the accumulated work of professionals — books, briefs, code, and analyses written by people who were never asked. When authors sued Anthropic over pirated books, the result was a $1.5 billion settlement, roughly $3,000 per book. But the court had already ruled that training on lawfully acquired books is fair use. The authors were paid once, for the piracy. The knowledge stays in the model. It is the oldest arrangement in this series: value generated by one class, captured by another.</p><h2>The Porter's Car</h2><p>Here the exercise turns. The sleeping car was the nineteenth century's new machine, and George Pullman staffed it almost entirely with Black men, many of them formerly enslaved. The job was a trap by design: brutal hours, low wages, dependence on tips, and a uniform that made every porter answer to the name "George."</p><p>The porters made it something else. In 1925 they organized the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters under A. Philip Randolph, and in 1937 they won the first major contract between a Black union and an American corporation. Along the way they carried the <em>Chicago Defender</em> south in their luggage, distributing the newspaper that helped launch the Great Migration. Porters' wages put children through college and anchored a Black middle class.</p><p>The lesson is structural. Every new machine needs a human interface — someone standing between the technology and the people it serves. Whoever organizes that layer captures value; whoever doesn't is just staff. AI has an interface layer too: the people who supervise it, verify it, sign for it, and decide what it's for. The question is whether they become organized porters or scattered staff.</p><h2>Wage, Moat, Deed</h2><p>Knowledge workers can hold value at three levels. Only one of them lasts.</p><p><strong>Wage.</strong> Experts are paid well right now to train AI — lawyers, doctors, and coders grading model outputs. It's good money, and it's temporary by design: once the model learns, the job ends, and the model keeps the knowledge forever. Freedmen got wages too. Wages alone built nothing durable.</p><p><strong>Moat.</strong> Licensing protects professions, and AI will not be admitted to the bar. But a license protects the job, not the ownership of the work. Since 2021 Arizona has licensed law firms with non-lawyer owners — more than 100 so far — and in 2025 KPMG became the first Big Four firm cleared to practice law in the United States. Each such firm needs a licensed compliance lawyer. The likely endgame: a few licensed lawyers, a fleet of AI, and investors holding the equity. The moat stays. The castle changes hands.</p><p><strong>Deed.</strong> Athletes didn't build wealth on talent alone, or on protection. They built it when they won ownership of their own rights: Curt Flood's challenge to the reserve clause, then free agency, then control of their names and likenesses. For knowledge workers, the deed takes three forms.</p><ul><li><strong>Rights, not fees.</strong> In 1914 songwriters formed ASCAP to license their work collectively, and they have collected royalties ever since. Professions could do the same with their expertise.</li><li><strong>Owning the firm.</strong> One professional with AI now has the reach of a small firm. The practice itself is the forty acres, and professionals should hold that equity before investors do.</li><li><strong>Accountability.</strong> AI cannot owe a fiduciary duty, lose a license, or be sued for malpractice. Someone must sign. That signature is permanently human, and it grows more valuable as the work beneath it multiplies.</li></ul><h2>Holding On</h2><p>Every previous wave of automation came with an escape hatch. The loom took the weaver's hands, so the weaver moved to the factory. The tractor took the farmhand's back, so the farmhand moved to the city. For two centuries the answer to automation was the same: think for a living. Now thinking is the thing being automated.</p><p>Even the deed is a defensive answer. It protects the first movers — the ones who organize, own, or sign. It doesn't answer the question underneath, which has to be asked plainly:</p><p><em>Fifty years from now, can we find any areas of growth for humans? Or are humans just holding on?</em></p><p>The fear isn't poverty. Societies have solved poverty before, unevenly and late. The fear is irrelevance — what the historian Yuval Noah Harari called the "useless class." It is one thing to be poor in a society that needs you. It is another to be comfortable in a society that doesn't.</p><p>And the stakes are political. Nearly every expansion of rights was purchased with leverage, and the leverage was usefulness. Kings conceded to parliaments because they needed taxes. Industrialists conceded to unions because a strike works only if the work stops. In 1865 the strongest argument for Black citizenship was the roughly 180,000 Black soldiers who had worn the Union uniform. The republic had needed them, and need created a debt.</p><p>Remove the need and you remove the leverage. States that draw their wealth from oil rather than citizens rarely answer to citizens. A state — or a company — that draws its wealth from compute faces the same temptation. The danger isn't that the machines won't need us. It's that the institutions won't.</p><p>If our only claim is usefulness, then yes, we are holding on, and the grip is slipping.</p><p>But usefulness was never the right claim.</p><h2>The Covenant</h2><p>Start with the smallest society: the family. No one earns a place there through output. The infant contributes nothing and receives everything. That is the original social organism, and its logic is nurture.</p><p>Nurture doesn't scale cheaply. Beyond the band and the village, strangers outnumber kin, and care gives way to protection. Societies scaled through duty: you owed your labor and your life upward, to God and king, and received protection in return. Empire was the troop enlarged. The British Empire, on which the sun never set, was structurally a dominance hierarchy with a navy.</p><p>Frustration with duty produced the rights-based society, and it is a real achievement: floors no king may breach. But it rests on a contract, and a contract is adversarial by design — two parties, each owing the other the minimum. Rights tell you what cannot be done to you. They are silent on what you are for. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks drew the line: a contract governs interests; a covenant binds people through belonging.</p><p>A nurture-based society — a covenant — starts from a different premise. Society exists not to extract a purpose from you, nor merely to referee your disputes, but to help you become whoever you can become, without defining that in advance. In Pixar's <em>Soul</em>, unborn souls must find a "spark" before entering life, and the hero assumes it must be a calling. The film's argument is that it was only ever readiness to live. A nurture-based society runs that seminar with no entrance exam.</p><p>The 1865 exercise shows what happens without it. Emancipation delivered rights without nurture. Sherman's Field Order 15 promised land; by the end of 1865 Andrew Johnson had begun handing it back to former Confederates. The freedmen held a contract and an empty hand, and the century that followed is the clearest evidence we have of what rights without nurture produce. Yet the cultural sovereignty that emerged grew precisely where nurture survived anyway: the church, the HBCU, the porters' union, the ballfield.</p><p>Now return to the fear. A society that no longer needs your labor is the first in history that can afford to care whether you flourish. Duty societies needed your body. Rights societies needed your work. The machine that threatens to make us unnecessary is also the surplus that makes nurture possible at scale. Whether we use it that way isn't a technical question. It's a choice about who holds the deed to the abundance.</p><p>What would humans do? We would be crafters, learners, entertainers, engineers, and explorers. Not because we'll do those things better than machines — we won't — but because human limits are the point. A computer beat the world chess champion in 1997, and chess is more popular now than ever. A marathon matters because the runner's body is failing. A novel matters because it was written by someone who will die, who doubted every page, and wrote it anyway. Constraint isn't a flaw in human output. It's the content.</p><p>And output stops being the purpose. Someone studies physics because the universe is astonishing; if she develops the polymer that makes an orbital skyhook possible, wonderful — but that's extra. Someone writes because writing makes them more fully themselves; if the book is extraordinary, that's extra too. Choices that would read as protest in a rights-based society read, in a nurture-based one, simply as a person becoming who they are.</p><p>One caution. "For your own good" was also the language of the boarding school and the civilizing mission. Nurture without rights is paternalism. So the covenant doesn't replace rights; it stands on them. Rights are the floor. Nurture is the house.</p><p>So are humans just holding on? Only if we keep asking to be needed. The better question is the one 1865 asked and answered a century too late: not whether society needs us, but whether it will nurture us. The future isn't empty. It's unnamed. And for the first time, it can afford us.</p>
