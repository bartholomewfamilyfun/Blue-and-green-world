<h2>The Bottom Rung</h2><p>For a century, the first years of a lawyer's career looked like drudgery. Document review. First drafts. Research memos no partner would read past page two. Firms billed it, associates resented it, and everyone understood the bottom rung is where young lawyers learned to climb to the top.</p><p>But now the bottom rung is being removed. Lateral associates — lawyers trained somewhere else, at someone else's expense — now make up 51% of associate hires at the country's 200 largest firms. Most big firms plan to grow their associate ranks through 2027, but only about a third plan to grow their first-year classes. They still want experienced lawyers. They just don't want to train them.</p><p>Law is the clearest case, not the only one; the same pattern runs through finance, consulting, software, and marketing. So: if no one climbs the bottom of the ladder, where does the top come from?</p><h2>Codified vs. Tacit</h2><p>The best evidence comes from payroll data. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab found that employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is now roughly 19% below where it would be had it tracked their peers in less-exposed jobs. Experienced workers in those same occupations show no comparable decline.</p><p>AI substitutes for codified knowledge — the kind written in textbooks and manuals. It does not yet substitute for tacit knowledge — the judgment built through years of doing. A company hires junior analysts and senior analysts. The machine can replace only one of them.</p><p>Unemployment among recent college graduates in summer 2026 sat within the range of the previous four years. Plenty of "AI layoffs" are ordinary cost-cutting in disguise. The displacement is real, but it isn't a flood. It's a drain: fewer people entering, not necessarily more people leaving. Drains are quieter than floods, and easier to ignore until the tank is empty.</p><h2>The Apprentice Clause</h2><p>In 1865, one of the first things several Southern legislatures did with freedom was regulate the ladder. Mississippi's Black Code included an apprenticeship law allowing courts to bind Black minors to masters — with preference given to their former owners. Training was the instrument of recapture. Whoever controlled the ladder controlled the class.</p><p>The jockeys tell the same story from the other direction. Thirteen of the fifteen riders in the first Kentucky Derby were Black. Black jockeys held the deepest expertise in the sport. Then racing grew lucrative, and within a generation they were gone from the Derby. Nothing about their skill had changed. What changed was who the skill was allowed to belong to.</p><p>Today's version is gentler, but structurally familiar. The models replacing junior professionals were trained on books, briefs, code, and analyses written by professionals who were never asked. When authors sued Anthropic over pirated books, the result was a $1.5 billion settlement, roughly $3,000 per book. But the court had already ruled that training on lawfully acquired books is fair use. The authors were paid once, for the piracy. The knowledge stays in the model. It is the oldest arrangement in this series: value generated by one class, captured by another.</p><h2>The Porter's Car</h2><p>Here the exercise turns. The sleeping car was the nineteenth century's new machine, and George Pullman staffed it almost entirely with Black men, many of them formerly enslaved. The job was a trap by design: brutal hours, low wages, dependence on tips, and a uniform that made every porter answer to the name "George." The forerunner to Hey Google, Siri, and Claude.</p><p>The porters made it something else. In 1925 they organized the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters under A. Philip Randolph, and in 1937 they won the first major contract between a Black union and an American corporation. Along the way they carried the <em>Chicago Defender</em> south in their luggage, distributing the newspaper that helped launch the Great Migration. Porters' wages put children through college and anchored a Black middle class.</p><p>The lesson is structural. Every new machine needs a human interface — someone standing between the technology and the people it serves. Whoever organizes that layer captures value; whoever doesn't is just staff. AI has an interface layer too: the people who supervise it, verify it, sign for it, and decide what it's for. The question is whether they become organized porters or scattered staff.</p><h2>Wage, Moat, Deed</h2><p>Knowledge workers can hold value at three levels. Only one of them lasts.</p><p><strong>Wage.</strong> Experts are paid well right now to train AI — lawyers, doctors, and coders grading model outputs. It's good money, and it's temporary by design. Once the model learns, the job ends, and the model keeps the knowledge forever. Freedmen got wages too. Wages alone built nothing durable.</p><p><strong>Moat.</strong> Licensing protects professions, and AI will not be admitted to the bar. But a license protects the job, not the ownership of the work. Since 2021 Arizona has licensed law firms with non-lawyer owners — more than 100 so far — and in 2025 KPMG became the first Big Four firm cleared to practice law in the United States. The likely endgame: a few licensed lawyers, a fleet of AI, and investors holding the equity. The moat stays. The castle changes hands.</p><p><strong>Deed.</strong> Athletes didn't build wealth on talent alone, or on protection. They built it when they won ownership of their own rights. Curt Flood challenged the reserve clause. Then players won free agency, then control of their names and likenesses. For knowledge workers, the deed takes three forms.</p><ul><li><p><strong>Rights, not fees.</strong> In 1914 songwriters formed ASCAP to license their work collectively, and they have collected royalties ever since. Professions could do the same with their expertise.</p></li><li><p><strong>Owning the firm.</strong> One professional with AI now has the reach of a small firm. The practice itself is the forty acres, and professionals should hold that equity before investors do.</p></li><li><p><strong>Accountability.</strong> AI cannot owe a fiduciary duty, lose a license, or be sued for malpractice. Someone must sign. That signature is permanently human, and it grows more valuable as the work beneath it multiplies.</p></li></ul><h2>Holding On</h2><p>Every previous wave of automation came with an escape hatch. The loom took the weaver's hands, so the weaver moved to the factory. The tractor took the farmhand's back, so the farmhand moved to the city. For two centuries the answer to automation was the same: think for a living. Now thinking is the thing being automated.</p><p>Even the deed is a defensive answer. It protects the first movers — the ones who organize, own, or sign. It doesn't answer the question underneath, which has to be asked plainly:</p><p><em>Fifty years from now, can we find any areas of growth for humans? Or are humans just holding on?</em></p><p>The fear isn't poverty. Societies have solved poverty before. The fear is irrelevance — what the historian Yuval Noah Harari called the "useless class." It is one thing to be poor in a society that needs you. It is another to be comfortable in a society that doesn't.</p><aside class="pullquote">The robots aren't coming for our jobs. They're coming for our serfdom.</aside><p>And the stakes are political. Nearly every expansion of rights was purchased with leverage, and the leverage was usefulness. Kings conceded to parliaments because they needed taxes. Industrialists conceded to unions because a strike works only if the work stops. In 1865 the strongest argument for Black citizenship was the roughly 180,000 Black soldiers who had worn the Union uniform. The republic had needed them, and need created a debt.</p><p>Remove the need and you remove the leverage. States that draw their wealth from oil rather than citizens rarely answer to citizens, and a state or company that draws its wealth from compute faces the same temptation. The danger isn't that the machines won't need us. It's that the institutions won't.</p><p>There is an irony here. For centuries, duty societies tried to turn people into machines — faceless Georges, punctual, interchangeable, tireless — and complained when people refused to stay that way. Now we are building actual machines to replace the lazy humans who never quite managed to behave like machines. That is why automation frightens us. We still think in the logic of a duty society, where your worth is your output. In that logic, the robots aren't coming for our jobs. They're coming for our serfdom.</p><p>If our only claim is usefulness, then yes, we are holding on, and the grip is slipping. But usefulness was never the right claim.</p><h2>The Covenant</h2><p>Start with the smallest society: the family. No one earns a place there through output. The infant contributes nothing and receives everything. That is the original social organism, and its logic is nurture.</p><p>Nurture doesn't scale cheaply. Beyond the band and the village, strangers outnumber kin, and care gives way to protection. Societies scaled through duty. You owed your labor and your life upward, to God and king, and received protection in return. The British Empire, on which the sun never set, was structurally <em>The Clan of the Cave Bear</em> with a navy.</p><p>Notice what happened in the scaling. The band existed to protect and feed the people in it. Duty reversed the arrow. Society became the thing to be served, and people became its parts — the serf, the soldier, the field hand, the factory hand — each valued for output. Strip away the steel and the satellites, and the organization looks familiar: an alpha, a hierarchy, a troop defending its territory. We built a space-age civilization and kept a primate org chart.</p><aside class="pullquote">Duty provides a sword to fight for society. Rights provide a shield to resist society.</aside><p>Frustration with duty produced the rights-based society, and it is a real achievement: walls no king may breach. But it rests on a contract, and a contract is adversarial by design — two parties, each owing the other the minimum.</p><p>Duty provides a sword to fight for society. Rights provide a shield to resist society. Neither ensures a sense of peace and ownership within society. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks drew the line: a contract governs interests; a covenant binds people through belonging.</p><p>A nurture-based society — a covenant — starts from a different premise. Society exists not to extract a purpose from you, nor merely to referee your disputes, but to help you become whoever you can become, without defining that in advance. In Pixar's <em>Soul</em>, unborn souls must find a "spark" before entering life. The hero assumes a spark must be a calling. The movie posits that it is only a readiness to live. A nurture-based society runs that seminar with no tuition or entrance exam.</p><p>The 1865 exercise shows what happens without it. Emancipation delivered rights without nurture. Sherman's Field Order 15 promised land; by the end of 1865 Andrew Johnson had begun handing it back to former Confederates. The freedmen held a contract and an empty hand. Cultural sovereignty emerged only from the cracks where nurture survived anyway: the church, the HBCU, the porters' union, the ballfield.</p><h2>The Fear or the Fulcrum</h2><p>Now return to the fear. A society that no longer needs your labor is the first in history that can afford to care whether you flourish. Duty societies needed your body. Rights societies needed your work. The machine that threatens to make us unnecessary can also provide the surplus that makes nurture possible at scale.</p><p>Whether we use it that way is a choice, and the first step is realizing there is one. Automation does not have to be a technology we mitigate while clinging to an ever-diminishing status quo. It can restore the original arrow: society as a machine that serves people, instead of people serving as the machine. None of this is guaranteed. But knowing the option exists should make us pursue it with exuberance, because make no mistake about it, a duty society with robots is a dystopia.</p><h2>Nurture Society?</h2><p>What would humans do? We would be crafters, learners, entertainers, engineers, and explorers. Not because we'll do those things better than machines, but because human limits are unknown. A computer beat the world chess champion in 1997, and chess is more popular now than ever. A marathon matters because the runner's body is failing. A novel matters because it was written by someone who will die, who doubted every page, and wrote it anyway. Constraint isn't a flaw in human output. It's an unknown quantity that makes our value limitless.</p><p>And output stops being the purpose. Someone studies physics because the universe is astonishing; if she develops the polymer that makes an orbital skyhook possible, wonderful — but that's extra. Someone writes because writing makes them more fully themselves; if the book is extraordinary, that's extra too.</p><h2>The Counterintuitive Outcome</h2><p>But pay attention to the counterintuitive outcome! In the nurture society there will be more scientists and more thinkers, not fewer. It's well documented how many brilliant minds drop out of STEM majors because of knockout courses and strict curves in grading. Does a person with encyclopedic memory make the doctor with the best bedside manner? Certainly not! Even in research, a creative C student could easily outdo the best memorizer at inventing new technology. On a macro scale, technology tends to accelerate because more people get to contribute to the brain trust.</p><p>In short, telling people they can study physics if they want is going to create more physicists than telling people that only the top 20% of physics students can get a job.</p><p>One caveat. I'm not talking about a <em>Man of Steel</em> Krypton, where the Codex assigns every person's role before birth. That's just the duty-based society with an extra helping of paternalism. The goal is self-actualization, with heavy emphasis on the self: society helping individuals reach their fullest potential, measured in personal satisfaction, contentment, and harmony with others.</p><p>So are humans just holding on? Only if we keep asking to be needed. The better question is how we design society to nurture us. The future isn't empty. It's unnamed. And for the first time, it can afford us.</p><p>This is Episode 3 of (we'll see 😉).</p><p>The 1865 Exercise — a series using history's biggest blind spot to connect with the technologies that scare us today. Episodes stand alone. Catch up anytime. 🚀</p>
